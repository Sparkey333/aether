# International Tactical & Reality-Based Systems

This cluster covers 20th–21st-century combat systems built explicitly for survival rather than sport: military close-combat doctrines (Krav Maga, Combat Sambo, WWII commando methods), national/ethnic survival systems (KAPAP, Hisardut, Defendo), the contested Russian art of Systema, the professional-protection trade (PMC/close-protection), and the broad commercial category of Reality-Based Self-Defense (RBSD). The defining tension throughout is that "reality-based" is a *claim*, not a *result* — several of these systems are genuinely battle-derived, while others borrow the aesthetics of operators and special forces to sell an experience that is rarely pressure-tested. Aether doctrine here: keep the marketing layer, but label it as marketing.

---

## Krav Maga

**Origin & people.** Krav Maga ("contact combat," Hebrew) was developed by **Imrich "Imi" Lichtenfeld / Imi Sde-Or** (b. 26 May 1910, Budapest; raised in Pozsony/Bratislava; d. 9 Jan 1998) (A). His father Samuel was a circus acrobat turned police self-defense instructor at the Hercules gym; Imi was a competitive boxer, wrestler, and gymnast (B). The foundational story — that he refined his fighting in 1930s street battles defending Jewish neighborhoods in Bratislava against antisemitic gangs, learning the gap between sport and street — is well attested (B/C). He reached Mandatory Palestine in 1942 via the British-supervised Czechoslovak 11th Infantry Battalion, began training **Haganah** and Palmach fighters from ~1944, and in 1948 became Chief Instructor for Physical Fitness and Krav Maga at the **IDF School of Combat Fitness**, serving ~20 years until retiring in 1964 (A/B). Post-retirement he civilianized the system, founding the **Israeli Krav Maga Association (IKMA)** on 22 Oct 1978 (A).

**Method/curriculum.** Aggression-first self-defense. Core pillars: simultaneous defense-and-attack, attacking vulnerable targets (eyes, throat, groin, knees), retzev ("continuous combat motion") of overwhelming strikes, weapon defenses (knife, stick, handgun, long gun), and disengagement to escape. Strikes are borrowed from boxing and Muay Thai; some clinch and ground from judo/wrestling/BJJ. Training emphasizes gross-motor techniques learnable fast, scenario drills, and stress inoculation.

**Evidence vs marketing.** The IDF lineage and Imi's biography are documented (A/B) — this is *not* a mythologized origin. The marketing problem is downstream: post-1990s commercial Krav Maga splintered into competing federations (see below), and many gyms sell short certifications, no live resistance, and "as taught to special forces" branding that overstates how much the current IDF actually trains hand-to-hand (D). Quality varies enormously by school.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: fast time-to-competence, excellent weapon-defense conceptual framework, strong against untrained aggressors, scalable to civilians. Cons: little-to-no sparring at many schools breeds false confidence; techniques degrade against a trained, resisting grappler; no competitive crucible to prune what doesn't work; brand fragmentation makes quality unpredictable.

**Integrations.** Practitioners routinely cross-train BJJ (ground gap), Muay Thai (striking depth), and combat shooting. Krav itself is a synthesis of boxing, wrestling, judo, and aikido-derived joint work.

### Civilian offshoots & federations
- **Krav Maga Worldwide (KMW)** — founded as a US licensing entity (Krav Maga Association of America, then KMW, ~1996–1999) by **Darren Levine**, among the first Americans to train under Imi (1981) and one of only two recipients of Imi's Founder's Diploma (A/B). Dominant US chain.
- **Krav Maga Global (KMG)** — founded 2010 by **Eyal Yanilov** (b. 1959), Imi's closest assistant and holder of Master Level 3 and the Founders Diploma of Excellence (1997); ~60+ countries, 1,500+ instructors (A/C).
- **IKMF (International Krav Maga Federation)** — lineage from Imi's 1990s international body. Multiple further splits (KMI, etc.) followed; lineage politics are intense and largely commercial (B).

## KAPAP

**Origin & people.** **KAPAP** (acronym for *Krav Panim el Panim*, "face-to-face combat") predates Krav Maga as a label. From roughly 1920–1948 it was the umbrella term for the Haganah/Palmach hand-to-hand and field-craft program drawing on boxing, judo, traditional jujutsu, wrestling, knife, and stick (B/C). Imi Lichtenfeld himself oversaw part of this program before his refinements came to be branded "Krav Maga" (B). Modern KAPAP organizations (notably under **Avi Nardia** and others) revived the name from the early 2000s as a distinct brand, marketing it as "the original Israeli martial art" (C).

**Method/curriculum.** Eclectic combatives: empty-hand striking and grappling, knife and stick (often with a strong filipino/kali flavor in modern versions), point-shooting, and instructor-development methodology. Modern KAPAP leans toward concept-based cross-training rather than a fixed syllabus.

**Evidence vs marketing.** The historical *KAPAP-as-pre-1948-program* is genuine (B). The claim that today's branded "KAPAP" is a continuous, intact transmission of that program is weaker — the modern revival is a reconstruction/rebrand assembled by contemporary instructors, not an unbroken lineage (C/D). "Original Israeli martial art" is technically defensible as a label but functions as marketing differentiation from Krav Maga.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: broad weapon literacy, instructor-friendly methodology, less dogmatic than some Krav lineages. Cons: brand more than system; quality and content vary by lead instructor; same sparring-deficit risk as Krav.

**Integrations.** Heavy overlap with Krav Maga, FMA (kali/escrima), and tactical firearms training.

## Hisardut (Survival)

**Origin & people.** **Hisardut** ("survival," Hebrew) was created by **Dennis Hanover** (b. South Africa, emigrated to Israel ~1960), a high-level karateka and jujutsuka who fused karate, judo, and jujutsu into a system taught from the 1960s–70s onward (B/C). From the early 1980s Hanover's curriculum was adopted by some IDF special-forces units seeking a deeper close-combat method than the basic conscript Krav Maga (C). His sons continued the lineage; the family ran a prominent dojo system in Israel.

**Method/curriculum.** More traditional-martial-arts structured than Krav: kata-influenced karate striking, judo throws, jujutsu locks and ground control, plus self-defense application and weapon defenses. Belt-ranked, longer development arc.

**Evidence vs marketing.** Hanover's credentials and the system's existence are solid (B). The "taught to Israeli special forces" claim is plausible and partially documented but, as with all Israeli systems, is amplified as a selling point (C/D). Less hyped than Krav overall.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: deeper technical base (real judo/karate underpinning), genuine grappling, ranked progression. Cons: longer learning curve than Krav's selling promise; smaller global footprint; harder to find outside Israel.

**Integrations.** Karate, judo, jujutsu roots; complements Krav Maga and KAPAP within the Israeli-combatives family.

## Systema

**Origin & people.** **Systema** ("the system," Russian) is the popular Western brand promoted by **Mikhail Ryabko** (Moscow, b. 1961) and his student **Vladimir Vasiliev**, who emigrated to Toronto and founded **Systema Headquarters** in 1993, the engine of the art's global spread (B/C). A parallel, more sport/technical Russian lineage is **ROSS** (Russian Native Martial Art) codified by **Alexander Retuinskih** in the early 1990s (B/C).

**Evidence vs marketing — read this carefully.**
- The **Cossack-origin claim** — that Systema descends from a 10th-century warrior art (sometimes dated to "948"), preserved through Cossacks/bogatyrs and "secret-keepers" through the Soviet era — is **(D) folklore/marketing-myth**. There is no documentary chain of transmission; Soviet policy actively suppressed pre-revolutionary martial traditions in favor of state systems (samoz, then sambo). Most historians treat modern Systema as a late-20th-century synthesis, not an ancient survival.
- The **"secret Spetsnaz / KGB art" claim** is **(D) hype** in its civilian-marketing form. Ryabko's biography (military/Interior Ministry service) is real (C), and *some* individuals with Systema backgrounds served — but the assertion that what is sold in commercial schools *is* the classified hand-to-hand of Russian special forces is unsupported. Actual military close-combat in Russia is sambo/army-combatives-based and not public.
- Investigative journalism (e.g. desk-russie, 2026) has additionally examined Systema clubs as potential nodes of Russian soft-power/influence networks abroad — a separate political concern from the technical claims, flagged here as reported allegation (B).

**Method/curriculum.** No fixed techniques, belts, or kata. Four "pillars": breathing, relaxation, posture/structure, continuous movement. Drills feature soft, flowing redirections, slow-work, ground movement, breath-led striking and "internal" power, knife flow, and stress/fear management. Distinctive aesthetic of relaxed practitioners absorbing strikes.

**Performance under pressure.** This is Systema's weak point. There is **no competition record** to validate it; demonstrations are overwhelmingly cooperative, and several have been criticized as "no-touch"/compliance theater (the "Fat Systema Guy" mockery of viral clips reflects this). Against a resisting opponent in any open-rules setting, Systema has no track record. Its breathing/relaxation/fear-management content has genuine value as movement and stress training; its combative efficacy is unproven (B/D).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: excellent breathing/relaxation and body-awareness work, low-impact and joint-friendly, useful adrenal/fear concepts, accessible to older bodies. Cons: zero pressure-testing or competition; mythologized origins; efficacy claims unverified; quality entirely instructor-dependent; political/influence questions around some clubs.

**Integrations.** Best paired with a pressure-tested base (boxing, BJJ, sambo) that supplies the realism Systema lacks; its breathing/relaxation methods can supplement any art.

## Combat Sambo

**Origin & people.** **Sambo** (*SAMozashchita Bez Oruzhiya*, "self-defense without weapons") was developed for the Soviet Red Army and police from the 1920s–30s. Two founders are credited: **Viktor Spiridonov** (WWI veteran; "samoz," emphasizing joint locks, off-balancing, leverage) and **Vasili Oshchepkov** (a judoka who trained directly under **Kanō Jigorō** in Japan, bringing throwing and live randori) (A/B). Their methods cross-pollinated into sambo, officially recognized as a Soviet sport on **16 Nov 1938** (USSR Sports Committee resolution no. 633) (A). Oshchepkov died in prison during the Great Purge (~1937) accused of being a Japanese spy (B). **Combat Sambo** is the strikes-included military/applied branch (sport sambo and beach sambo being the others).

**Method/curriculum.** Combat Sambo permits punches, kicks, knees, elbows, headbutts, throws, and ground submissions (notably leg locks, a sambo signature) — an MMA-like ruleset with protective headgear. Governed internationally by **FIAS**; world championships draw athletes from ~80 countries (B). Training is fully resisted and competitive.

**Evidence vs marketing.** Among the strongest evidence base in this cluster. Combat Sambo is pressure-tested by definition and has a documented combat-sport pedigree feeding directly into elite MMA: **Fedor Emelianenko** (multiple-time Combat Sambo world champion) and **Khabib Nurmagomedov** (multi-time world champion) are products of this system (B). The Soviet-military origin is real history, not branding.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: genuinely battle/competition-tested; elite throwing + leg-lock game; striking integrated with grappling; proven MMA crossover. Cons: high physical demand and injury risk; outside Russia/Eastern Europe schools are scarce; weapon and multi-attacker work is limited vs. dedicated combatives.

**Integrations.** Direct MMA pipeline; complements judo, wrestling, BJJ, and boxing.

## British Commando Close-Combat (WWII) & "Combat 56"

**Origin & people.** The canonical WWII British system was codified by **William E. Fairbairn** and **Eric A. Sykes**, ex-**Shanghai Municipal Police** officers who distilled "Defendu"/close-quarters methods from years of street violence in 1920s–30s Shanghai (A/B). From 1940 they trained British Commandos, the SOE, and (via Fairbairn) early American OSS units; the **Fairbairn–Sykes fighting knife** (first batch ~Jan 1941, Wilkinson Sword) is the iconic artifact (A). Doctrine: brutal simplicity — edge-of-hand blows, knee/elbow strikes, gouges, the knife as a thrusting weapon, point-shooting, and the principle that there is "no fair play" in war.

**"Combat 56."** Caution on naming: searches return **two distinct things**. (1) A **Polish** system "Combat 56," credited to Major Arkadiusz Kups of the 56th Special Company (C). (2) In British circles, ex-**SAS** instructor **Bob Spour** teaches "SAS Fighting Secrets"/WWII-combatives-derived material (pre-emptive striking, palm strikes, elbows, head control) (C). The user's "British Combat 56" appears to conflate the SAS-combatives revival scene with the separate Polish system — flagged so the record stays honest (B/D).

**Method/curriculum.** Gross-motor lethal techniques for soldiers under stress and fatigue: few moves, high repetition, no sport rules, integrated with the knife and sidearm. Modern revivalists (Spour and others) teach these with minimal modification.

**Evidence vs marketing.** The WWII pedigree is rigorously documented (A/B) — Fairbairn and Sykes are primary-source history. The marketing risk is in modern "SAS secrets" branding, which sells the lineage's glamour; the techniques are sound but simple, and "secret" is overstated since the manuals (*Get Tough*, *All-In Fighting*) are published (B/D).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: combat-proven simplicity, fast to train, weapon-integrated, honest about lethal intent. Cons: designed for war not legally-bounded civilian self-defense; little live grappling; some modern courses trade on mystique; not a complete unarmed art.

**Integrations.** Foundation for most Western military combatives; pairs with combat shooting and edged-weapon training.

## Defendo

**Origin & people.** **Defendo** descends from **Combato**, an unarmed system created by English-born Canadian **Bill Underwood** (b. 1895, England; immigrated to Canada 1910; d. 8 Feb 1986), who drew on jiu-jitsu learned from Japanese masters in Liverpool (B/C). Underwood became Canada's leading WWII unarmed-combat instructor. Because Combato was too aggressive for police use, the de-escalated, compliance/escort-oriented public version **Defendo** was named on 15 Aug 1945 (by Underwood's daughter Pat) (B/C). A 1980 documentary, *Don't Mess With Bill*, was Oscar-nominated (1981) (A). The Defendo name later branched (e.g. Scandinavian "Defendo" lineages under other instructors).

**Method/curriculum.** Practical, low-flair self-defense and arrest-and-control: joint manipulation, off-balancing, escort/come-along holds, and simple strikes — engineered for law-enforcement and civilian use rather than battlefield lethality.

**Evidence vs marketing.** Solid, well-documented mid-century combatives lineage (A/B); relatively un-hyped. The main caveat is that "Defendo" is now a name used by several unrelated modern organizations of varying quality (C).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: legally-minded control techniques, practical for police/security, genuine historical pedigree. Cons: less striking depth; fragmented modern branding; limited live-resistance training in some schools.

**Integrations.** Aligns with police defensive tactics, judo/jujutsu, and other WWII-era combatives.

## Modern PMC / Close-Protection Training

**Origin & people.** Not a "martial art" but a **professional training category** — the curricula used by close-protection officers (CPOs/bodyguards), private military contractors (PMCs), and executive-protection teams. It crystallized from military/special-forces methods commercialized post-Cold War and accelerated after the 2000s Iraq/Afghanistan PMC boom; no single founder (B). Delivered by academies worldwide (often staffed by ex-military/police).

**Method/curriculum.** Integrated and scenario-driven rather than style-based: **CQB** (room/vehicle/stairwell movement, angle control), defensive tactics/hand-to-hand for control and disengagement, protective formations and motorcade drills, route planning and threat assessment, counter-surveillance, medical (TCCC), and live-fire/force-on-force with Simunition. Hand-to-hand is a *minor* component — usually a control-and-create-distance set borrowed from Krav, BJJ, judo, or military combatives.

**Evidence vs marketing.** The serious end (force-on-force, live-fire, Simunition CQB villages) is genuinely pressure-tested and operationally grounded (B). The marketing risk is the proliferation of short "tactical" courses selling operator aesthetics; unarmed-combat content is often thin, and "PMC-grade" branding is heavily commoditized (D).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: realistic scenario and force-on-force training, weapon-integrated, profession-relevant. Cons: not a complete unarmed system; quality varies wildly by provider; expensive; the brand "tactical" is heavily diluted.

**Integrations.** Combat shooting, Krav Maga / military combatives for the empty-hand layer, BJJ for control, medical and driving skills.

## Reality-Based Self-Defense (RBSD) — the category

**Origin & people.** **RBSD** is an umbrella category, not one system, coalescing in the 1980s–90s around instructors who prioritized real assault dynamics over sport or tradition. Key figures: **Geoff Thompson** (UK; nightclub-doorman experience; popularized "the fence," pre-emption, and adrenal "Animal Day" drills; co-founded the British Combat Association) (B); **Tony Blauer** (**SPEAR System**, built on the startle-flinch reflex and fear management) (B/C); **Tim Larkin** (**Target Focus Training**, injury-focused "asocial violence" — notably banned from entering the UK in 2012 over his methods) (B). Krav Maga, KAPAP, and many commando-derived schools also market under the RBSD banner.

**Method/curriculum.** Common threads: threat awareness and avoidance, verbal de-escalation, pre-emptive striking, gross-motor techniques under adrenaline, weapon and multiple-attacker scenarios, and "scenario/pressure-testing" drills with padded aggressors.

**Evidence vs marketing — the core critique.** RBSD's honest insight is real: legal, ambush, multi-attacker, and weapon contexts that sport arts ignore. But "pressure testing" is the category's most abused term. In many RBSD schools it means calisthenics-to-exhaustion plus shouting and padded-suit choreography — adrenaline simulation, not resisted fighting against a skilled opponent (B). The strongest practitioners pair RBSD's scenario/legal/awareness layer with a genuinely resisted base (boxing, wrestling, BJJ, sambo); the weakest sell adrenaline theater and unverifiable lethal techniques. Treat any "too deadly to spar / too deadly for the ring" claim as a red flag (D).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: addresses awareness, de-escalation, legal/ambush/weapon realities sport ignores; fast gross-motor focus; psychologically realistic at its best. Cons: "pressure testing" frequently fake; many techniques never tested against resistance; "too deadly to spar" excuse-making; enormous quality variance; ripe for charlatanism.

**Integrations.** Designed to layer on top of a resisted combat sport — its scenario, legal, and awareness content complements BJJ/boxing/Muay Thai/sambo, which supply the realism.
