# Ancient & Civilizational Roots — Pankration to Beni Hasan

This is the deep-history layer of the Codex: the oldest *documented* fighting arts, anchored wherever possible to archaeology and primary documents rather than lineage romance. The governing principle here is Aether's "keep every layer, label every layer" — we separate what tomb murals, reliefs, vases, and surviving texts actually show **(A)** from scholarly reconstruction **(B)**, living-lineage oral history **(C)**, and national-pride folklore **(D)**. Almost everything in this cluster scores *low* on accessibility: these are extinct or heavily reconstructed systems, surviving as evidence, not as schools you can walk into.

A recurring caution: combat *sport* and combat *art* are over-represented in the record because elites commissioned the murals, vases, and arenas that survive. Battlefield killing methods — the actual military core — were rarely illustrated and almost never written down, so the evidentiary picture skews toward grappling and dueling.

---

## Greek Pankration (παγκράτιον)

**Primary evidence.** Pankration ("all-power") entered the Olympics at the **33rd Olympiad, 648 BC (A)** — the date is fixed by the *Olympionikai* victor lists and corroborated by Pausanias and Philostratus. Vase paintings (Attic black- and red-figure, 6th–5th c. BC) show the two recognized modes: *ano* (upright) and *kato* (ground) pankration (A). The mythic founders were Heracles and Theseus (D).

**Mechanics.** A near-no-rules blend of *pygmachia* (boxing) and *pale* (wrestling): strikes, kicks, throws, joint locks, and chokes were all legal; only biting and eye-gouging (and finger-into-orifice attacks) were barred (A/B). Victory came by submission (the *apagoreuein* finger-raise), unconsciousness, or death. Ground fighting was central — the closest documented ancient analogue to modern MMA.

**Living descendants.** No unbroken lineage; modern "pankration" federations are 20th–21st-c. reconstructions/marketing (D). Its conceptual heir is mixed martial arts, which independently rediscovered the strike+grapple synthesis.

**Caveat.** Reconstructions claiming preserved technical curricula are not credible (D); we have *outcomes and rules*, not a manual.

## Greek Pygmachia / Pygmē (Boxing) & Pale (Wrestling)

**Primary evidence.** Boxing entered the Olympics at the **23rd Olympiad, 688 BC (A)** (winner traditionally Onomastos of Smyrna). The deepest physical evidence predates the Greeks: the **Minoan Boxer Rhyton / "Boxer Vase" from Akrotiri-Hagia Triada, Crete, c. 1500 BC (A)**, and the Akrotiri "Boxing Boys" fresco, Thera, c. 1600 BC (A) — children already wearing a single glove. The **Boxer at Rest / Terme Boxer bronze (c. 100–50 BC) (A)** shows the *himantes oxeis* (sharp leather thongs) and a face of cumulative trauma — cauliflower ears, broken nose.

**Mechanics.** No weight classes, no rings, no rounds; fighters wrapped hands in *himantes* (soft thongs, later hardened "sharp" versions), fought to submission or KO, and aimed almost exclusively at the head (B). Wrestling (*pale*) was won by three throws; it was the most *technical* and respected of the heavy events.

**Living descendants.** Modern boxing and freestyle/Greco-Roman wrestling are conceptual, not lineal, heirs (B). Greco-Roman wrestling's name is a 19th-c. French romantic invention, not a Greek survival (D).

## Roman Military Combat & Gladiatorial Fighting

**Primary evidence.** The **gladiatorial *ludus*** is archaeologically excavated — the Ludus Magnus beside the Colosseum, and the Pompeii gladiator barracks with surviving arms (A). Mosaics, reliefs, and tombstones depict the typed fighter classes (*murmillo, retiarius, thraex, secutor*) (A). For the army, **Vegetius's *De Re Militari* (c. 4th–5th c. AD) (A)** preserves the training method: recruits drilled against the *palus* (a 6-Roman-foot wooden post) with wooden swords and wicker shields **double the weight** of real gear, "morning and afternoon."

**Mechanics.** Roman *military* combat was a system of disciplined close-order *gladius* thrusting behind the *scutum*, not individual flourish — short stabs to the body over slashing (B). Gladiatorial combat was a *regulated spectacle* with referees (*summa rudis*), paired matchups, and surprisingly low fatality per bout for trained, valuable fighters (B).

**Living descendants.** No continuous lineage; HEMA and modern reconstruction draw on Vegetius and archaeology (C/B). "Gladiator workout" branding is pure marketing (D).

**Caveat.** Distinguish arena sport (well-documented) from battlefield method (inferred). Bone evidence from the **Ephesus gladiator cemetery (A)** confirms standardized weapon wounds and skilled medical care.

## Egyptian Wrestling — Beni Hasan

**Primary evidence.** The single richest combat archive of the ancient world: the **Beni Hasan tomb murals, Middle Kingdom, 11th–12th Dynasty, c. 2000–1900 BC (A)**. **Tomb 15 (Baqet III)** carries roughly **400+ paired wrestlers**, and **Tomb 17 (Khety)** several hundred more, each pair painted in differentiated colors so the limbs of the two fighters can be told apart across a full sequence of holds, takedowns, and ground control (A).

**Mechanics.** The sequences read almost like a modern instructional flipbook — body locks, hip throws, leg trips, gut-wrenches, and pins — demonstrating a *systematic* curriculum, not random brawling (B). This is arguably the earliest depicted fighting *system* on Earth.

**Living descendants.** Nubian/Sudanese and broader African folk wrestling preserve broadly similar belt/standing grappling (C), but no proven unbroken technical descent (B).

**Caveat.** The murals show holds vividly but no text explains rules, scoring, or whether this was sport, military drill, or funerary symbolism — likely all three (B).

## Egyptian Tahtib (Stick-Fighting)

**Primary evidence.** Reliefs at **Abusir (Pyramid/mortuary temple of Sahure, 5th Dynasty, c. 2500 BC) (A)** show staff drill alongside archery and wrestling; further depictions at **Beni Hasan (c. 1900–1700 BC)** and the **Amarna talatat (c. 1350 BC) (A)** show paired stick contestants, and by the New Kingdom tahtib appears in Luxor/Saqqara festival processions (A).

**Mechanics.** Single long staff (~4 ft), emphasizing a high guard, controlled strikes to the head, and parries (B). It transitioned over millennia from martial drill to a still-living folk **dance/sport of Upper Egypt**, inscribed by **UNESCO (2016)** as intangible heritage (A).

**Living descendant.** Modern *tahtib* (and its dance form *raqs al-assaya*) is a genuinely continuous folk practice (C) — one of the few entries here with a living thread, even if the combat application has softened.

## Mesopotamian / Sumerian Grappling

**Primary evidence.** The **Khafajah bronze/copper statuette (Nintu Temple, Diyala, c. 2600–2370 BC) (A)** depicts two wrestlers each gripping the other's belt/girdle — direct evidence of **belt wrestling**. The **Epic of Gilgamesh (Old Babylonian text, c. 1800 BC, on earlier Sumerian material) (A)** describes Gilgamesh and Enkidu wrestling: a grip-and-lift over the hip, "knee bent," matching the statuette's stance.

**Mechanics.** Belt-grip standing wrestling with throws by lifting and hip rotation (B) — a form structurally echoed today across Central Asian, Turkic, and Caucasian belt-wrestling.

**Living descendants.** No lineal survival; the belt-wrestling *family* (kurash, koshti, etc.) is a parallel, not a proven descent (C/D).

**Caveat.** The literary scene is poetic, not a technical manual; the statuette is the harder evidence (A).

## Indian Malla-yuddha, Vajra-Mushti & Dhanurveda

**Primary evidence.** Textual rather than archaeological. **Malla-yuddha** (combat wrestling) appears in epic and Puranic literature; the technical **Malla Purana (Gujarat, c. 13th c. AD) (A)** classifies wrestler types, diets, exercises, and pit preparation. **Dhanurveda** (the "science of archery"/martial science) survives first in the **Agni Purana (c. 8th–11th c. AD) (A)**. **Vajra-Mushti** ("thunderbolt-fist," using a knuckleduster) is first named in the **Manasollasa of Someshvara III (1124–1138 AD) (A)**, with conjectured but unproven Mauryan-era roots (C).

**Mechanics.** Malla-yuddha integrated throws, joint-breaking, strikes, chokes, and pressure points, graded into four intensities from sport to lethal (B). Dhanurveda framed weapons and unarmed combat within a broader cosmological discipline.

**Living descendants.** Strong living threads: **kushti/pehlwani** wrestling, **kalaripayattu** (Kerala), and surviving **vajra-mushti** demonstrations among Jyesthimalla wrestlers (C).

**Caveat.** Claims that these arts are 5,000 years old, or seeded all Asian martial arts via Bodhidharma, are **national-pride embellishment (D)**; the hard datable texts are medieval.

## Chinese Jiao Li / Jueli (角力) & Jiao Di (角抵)

**Primary evidence.** **Jiao Li / Jueli** is named in the **Classic of Rites (Liji), compiled Zhou–Han, c. 3rd–1st c. BC (A)** as a military training discipline. The older term **Jiao Di** ("horn-butting") is attested in Qin–Han texts and a famous **Western Han painted lacquer comb / wooden tomb plaques (A)** depicting two wrestlers refereed by a third.

**Mechanics.** Jiao Li combined throws with strikes, blocks, joint locks, and pressure-point attacks within an explicitly *military* curriculum (B). Jiao Di evolved from a horned-headgear butting contest into refereed wrestling.

**Living descendant.** **Shuai Jiao** is the genuine modern continuation of this throwing lineage (B/C).

**Caveat.** The **Yellow Emperor vs. Chi You (2697 BC)** origin is **legend (D)** — there is no archaeology for a 3rd-millennium-BC martial system; the firm record is Zhou/Han.

## Japanese Ancient Sumo (Sumai)

**Primary evidence.** The **Nihon Shoki (compiled 720 AD) (A)** records the *legendary* first mortal bout — **Nomi no Sukune vs. Taima no Kuehaya, dated to 23 BC** — fought to the death (legend: C/D). **Kofun-period haniwa figures of wrestlers (c. 300–538 AD) (A)** give physical evidence, and the **first *historically attested* sumo was held in 642 AD** at Empress Kōgyoku's court for a Korean legation (A).

**Mechanics.** Originally a brutal, often lethal *sumai* with strikes and throws, later ritualized under Shinto, codified into ring-out/touchdown rules during the Nara–Heian courts (B).

**Living descendant.** Modern professional **ōzumō** is directly continuous (B) — among the most evidence-supported living lines in this cluster.

**Caveat.** The 23 BC date is a *text composed ~740 years later* about a *mythologized* event; treat it as legend, not as a 1st-c.-BC datum (C/D).

## Norse / Viking Glíma & Holmgang

**Primary evidence.** Documentary, from the **Icelandic sagas (written 13th c. AD about the 9th–11th c.) (A)** — *Grettis saga*, *Kjalnesinga saga* (the *fang-hella* "back-breaking stone"), and dueling accounts in *Egils saga* and *Kormáks saga*. The **Grágás law code (A)** and saga rules describe **holmgang** (formal duel) on a pegged ox-hide or small island (*holm*).

**Mechanics.** **Glíma** is jacket/belt-grip wrestling emphasizing upright throws and balance; *lausatök* ("free grip") was the rougher combat form (B). **Holmgang** was legally sanctioned single combat with prescribed weapons, shields, and turn-taking strikes — a judicial duel, not free-form melee.

**Living descendant.** Icelandic **glíma** survives continuously and is still practiced competitively (C) — a rare living link here.

**Caveat.** Sagas were written 200–300 years after the events, blending memory and literary craft; treat fight choreography as plausible reconstruction (B), not transcript.

## Mesoamerican (Aztec / Maya) Ritual Combat

**Primary evidence.** Aztec **gladiatorial sacrifice (*tlahuahuanaliztli*)** during the **Tlacaxipehualiztli festival**, dedicated to Xipe Totec, is described in **16th-c. codices and Sahagún's *Florentine Codex* (A)** and shown in pictorial manuscripts: a captive tethered to a round **temalacatl** stone, armed with a feather-edged mock club, facing eagle/jaguar warriors with obsidian-edged *macuahuitl* (A). Maya circular altars resembling the temalacatl suggest parallel rites (B).

**Mechanics.** This was *ritual asymmetric combat* — a spectacle of sacrifice, not a fair sport; survival was essentially impossible by design (B). Broader Maya/Aztec warrior training in spear, atlatl, club, and shield is inferred from iconography (B).

**Living descendants.** None continuous; the *macuahuitl* and warrior culture are reconstructed from codices and conquistador accounts (C). No empty-hand "Aztec martial art" lineage survives.

**Caveat.** Heavy reliance on **post-Conquest Spanish-mediated sources**, which carry observer bias (B); pre-Conquest material is iconographic.

## Ancient African (Nuba) Wrestling

**Primary evidence.** **Nuba wrestling** of the Kordofan hills (Sudan) is documented ethnographically — most famously **Leni Riefenstahl's *The Last of the Nuba* (1973) and *People of Kau* (1976) (A as photographic record)** — and is plausibly linked to **Nubian/Kushite wrestling traditions** of the broader Nile, themselves echoed in Egyptian murals (B/C). Pharaonic reliefs depict Nubian wrestlers in tribute games (A), supporting deep antiquity in the *region* if not strict continuity.

**Mechanics.** Standing grappling for the throw/takedown, traditionally performed nearly nude with ash/ornament, embedded in harvest ritual and male initiation (B/C).

**Living descendant.** Nuba wrestling is **living and continuous** today, sustained even in displacement/refugee communities (C) — among the most authentically continuous folk arts here.

**Caveat.** The "direct descent from pharaonic Egypt" claim is attractive but **unproven (C/D)**; what is solid is the region's millennia-deep wrestling culture.

## Persian / Parthian Martial Tradition

**Primary evidence.** Persian arms (composite bow, akinakes) and the famed **"Parthian shot"** (firing backward from a galloping horse) are attested in Greek/Roman sources and Parthian art (A). The *empty-hand* tradition crystallizes later in the **zurkhāneh ("house of strength") / varzesh-e bāstāni** system, whose **Mithraic-temple architecture and koshti pahlavani wrestling are traditionally traced to the Parthian era (247 BC–224 AD) (C)**; the term *pahlavan* derives from "Pahlav" (Parthian) (B).

**Mechanics.** A fusion of strength training (heavy clubs *meels*, *kabbadeh* bow-tensioner, *sang* shields), wrestling (*koshti*), and ethical/spiritual discipline set to drum and chant (B). Battlefield Persia, by contrast, prized cavalry archery above grappling.

**Living descendant.** **Zurkhaneh / Pahlevani rituals** survive continuously and are **UNESCO-listed (2010)** (A/C); **koshti** wrestling remains Iran's national sport (C).

**Caveat.** Direct Parthian-to-zurkhaneh continuity is **lineage tradition, not documented (C/D)**; the surviving system is medieval-to-modern in its attested form.

## Korean Subak / Taekkyeon

**Primary evidence.** The **Goguryeo tomb murals** are the anchor: **Gakjeochong ("Wrestlers' Tomb") and Muyongchong ("Tomb of the Dancers"), c. 4th–5th c. AD (A)**, depict belt-wrestling (*ssireum*-like) and paired empty-hand figures often read as early **subak/taekkyeon** (B). Textually, **subak** appears in *Goryeosa* (Goryeo history), and the **Muyedobotongji (1790) (A)** is the major surviving Korean military arts manual — though it is largely Chinese-derived and **does not cover taekkyeon**.

**Mechanics.** Taekkyeon is a rhythmic, fluid kicking/tripping/pushing art with a distinctive springy "pum-balki" footwork; subak referenced earlier hand-and-foot fighting (B).

**Living descendant.** **Taekkyeon** survives, **UNESCO-listed (2011)** as intangible heritage (A/C); it is *not* the ancestor of modern Taekwondo, which is a mid-20th-c. synthesis heavily shaped by Japanese karate (B).

**Caveat.** Reading the murals as proof of a continuous taekwondo lineage to antiquity is **national-pride embellishment (D)**; the murals show *that* people grappled and kicked, not a named, transmitted system.
