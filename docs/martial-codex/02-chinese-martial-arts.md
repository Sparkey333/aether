# Chinese Martial Arts — External Kung Fu, Internal Arts & JKD

Chinese martial arts (*wushu* / *kung fu*, properly *gōngfū* "skill from effort") span a 3,000+ year continuum from battlefield wrestling and spear drill to temple-bound monastic exercise, family-secret village systems, and modern stage performance. This codex section keeps every layer and labels every layer: it separates documented history (A/B) from lineage oral tradition (C) and romantic origin myth (D), and is honest about the hard split between *combat-tested* CMA (Sanda, Shuai Jiao, JKD-trapping-into-MMA) and the much larger world of forms, health practice, and demonstration wushu that underperforms badly under full-contact pressure. Where an art is mythologized as "ancient" or "supreme," that marketing is flagged (D) while still reported.

---

## Shuai Jiao / Jiao Li (Chinese Wrestling) — the root layer

**Root source & history.** Shuai Jiao (摔跤, "throw-grab") is the codified descendant of *Jiao Li* (角力) and the legendary *Jiao Di* (角抵), grappling drills attested in Zhou-dynasty military texts and listed in the *Rites of Zhou* (~3rd c. BCE) (B). The popular "4,000-year-old, oldest martial art on Earth" claim — tied to the mythic Yellow Emperor's troops wearing horned helmets to gore enemies — is folklore/marketing (D). The modern jacket-wrestling form was standardized by the Qing imperial *Shan Pu Ying* (Battalion of Excellency in Catching) at the Manchu court (B), absorbing Mongolian *böke* wrestling.

**Lineage.** Parent of essentially all Chinese throwing/clinch method; feeds the takedown game of Sanda, and historically the close-range of many *quan* systems. Regional branches: Beijing, Tianjin, Baoding (Hebei) styles.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Upright jacket grappling: grip the lapel/sleeve, off-balance, and throw in one explosive beat. No ground-fighting phase — a throw scores and resets. Sweeps, hip throws, leg-pick takedowns, *kao* shoulder-bumps.

**Training methodology.** Live throwing on partners, neck/grip-strength conditioning, stance-walking with a weighted bar (*shuai jiao gan*). Heavy resistance from day one — this is the part of CMA that is genuinely pressure-tested.

**Pros & cons.** Pro: real, alive, transferable takedowns; the clinch backbone Sanda and CMA needed. Con: no submissions, minimal groundwork, smaller modern competition footprint than judo.

**Integrates with.** Sanda, judo, MMA wrestling; complements any striking art lacking clinch.

## Shaolin Kung Fu — Northern Changquan vs Southern Nanquan

**Root source & history.** The Shaolin Monastery (少林寺) on Mt. Song, Henan, was founded ~495–496 CE (B). The famous origin story — that the Indian monk **Bodhidharma** (Damo) arrived ~5th–6th c. CE, taught the monks the *Yijin Jing* exercises, and thereby fathered Shaolin kung fu — is a debunked 17th-century literary invention traced to the *Yijin Jing* qigong manual; martial-arts historians (Henning, Shahar) classify it as apocryphal legend (D). Documented monastic fighting (notably staff method against bandits and pirates) is solid from the Ming dynasty (16th–17th c.) onward (B). The "Southern Shaolin Temple in Fujian," cradle of the southern styles and the Five Elders/anti-Qing rebellion narrative, has no confirmed archaeological site and is largely lineage tradition (C)/legend (D).

**Lineage.** Shaolin is less a single style than an umbrella and a brand. **Northern (Changquan, "Long Fist")**: long-range, extended postures, high kicks, leaping, large frames — the technical ancestor of demonstration wushu Changquan and of Northern Praying Mantis, Eagle Claw, etc. **Southern (Nanquan, "Southern Fist")**: short-bridge hand power, rooted stances, minimal kicking — parent of Hung Gar, Choy Li Fut, Wing Chun, Bak Mei, and the Hakka/Cantonese family arts.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Northern: *tornado kicks*, butterfly kicks, sweeping low stances (*pubu*), animal forms (five animals: tiger, crane, leopard, snake, dragon). Southern: *kiu sao* bridge hands, iron-palm/iron-body conditioning, sticky short-range power, the "sound" (*fa sheng*) shout.

**Training methodology.** Stance-holding (*zhan zhuang* and *mabu*), form (*taolu*) memorization, conditioning (iron palm, iron shirt), staff and spear. Today: a spectrum from genuine monastery/wushu-school athleticism to tourist-show acrobatics.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: deep athletic foundation, vast technical vocabulary, cultural depth, spectacular. Cons: forms-heavy, light on alive sparring; demonstration Shaolin has weak full-contact transfer; brand diluted by performance troupes.

**Integrates with.** Sanda (for live application), modern wushu, and the entire southern-family tree below.

## Wing Chun (詠春)

**Root source & history.** Origin is essentially undocumented before the 19th century. The famous legend — the Shaolin nun **Ng Mui** (one of the Five Elders) created it after watching a crane fight a snake, then taught the young woman **Yim Wing-chun**, who used it to repel a warlord suitor — is oral tradition with no contemporary record, best tagged (C)/(D). Scholarly view: it likely coalesced in southern China (Guangdong/Fujian) in the 18th–19th century (B). Globalization is firmly historical: **Ip Man** (1893–1972) (A) standardized and exported it from Foshan to Hong Kong after 1949; his student **Bruce Lee** made it world-famous.

**Lineage.** Parent: Southern Shaolin / Hakka short-bridge arts (traditional). Branches: Ip Man lineage (Wong Shun-leung, Bruce Lee, Moy Yat, Ip Ching/Ip Chun) plus older/parallel lines (Yuen Kay-shan, Gulao/Pien San, Pan Nam, Hung Suen/Red Boat).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Centerline theory, simultaneous attack-and-defense, economy of motion, the vertical chain-punch, *tan sao / bong sao / fook sao* deflections, low front/side kicks, and the *biu jee* finishing concepts. Three empty-hand forms (*Siu Nim Tao, Chum Kiu, Biu Jee*), the wooden dummy (*muk yan jong*), plus pole and butterfly swords.

**Training methodology.** Forms, then **chi sao** ("sticky hands") — tactile reflex training from a clinch-touch range. Strength is the touch-sensitivity drilling; weakness is that chi sao is often mistaken for sparring.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: fast, efficient, close-range, accessible, low physical demand. Cons: thin against grapplers and long-range kickers; chi sao ≠ live resistance; poor record in honest full-contact tests.

**Integrates with.** **Jeet Kune Do** (its direct parent), boxing, and increasingly MMA hybrid programs that pressure-test its trapping.

## Hung Gar (洪家)

**Root source & history.** A southern Shaolin "family" style; traditionally founded by **Hung Hei-gun** (Hung Hei-koon) in the 18th century, said to be a tea-merchant student of the Southern Shaolin monk Gee Sin (C)/(D). Historically solid from **Wong Fei-hung** (1847–1925) (A/B), the Guangzhou physician and folk hero who systematized it and choreographed the *Tiger-Crane Paired Form*; transmitted onward by Lam Sai-wing.

**Lineage.** Parent: Southern Shaolin / the "Five Family Fists" (Hung, Lau, Choy, Lei, Mok). Branches: Wong Fei-hung line via Lam Sai-wing / Lam Cho; Tang Fung line.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Tiger-claw grabbing power + crane precision; deep rooted *si ping ma* horse stance; bridge-hand conditioning; the five-animal/five-element theory. Signature forms: *Gung Gee Fook Fu* (Taming the Tiger), *Fu Hok Seung Ying* (Tiger-Crane), *Tiet Sin Kuen* (Iron Wire, an internal/breathing form).

**Training methodology.** Long static stance work, iron-body/iron-arm conditioning, slow tension-breathing in Iron Wire. Builds tremendous structural strength; light on mobility and live sparring.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: powerful close-range structure, strong grip/clinch, great conditioning and health element. Cons: stance-bound, slow to mobile attackers, conditioning-heavy with injury risk, limited competition outlet.

**Integrates with.** Other southern systems, Choy Li Fut (regional rivalry but complementary), Sanda for application.

## Choy Li Fut (蔡李佛)

**Root source & history.** One of the most precisely dated CMA: formally founded **1836** by **Chan Heung** in King Mui village, Guangdong (B). Named to honor his three teachers — the monk **Choy Fook** (Choy Gar), **Li Yau-san** (Li Gar), and his uncle **Chan Yuen-woo** (Fut/Buddha Gar) — making it an explicit synthesis art. Carried strong anti-Qing revolutionary associations.

**Lineage.** Parents: Choy Gar + Li Gar + Fut Gar (southern Shaolin lines). Branches: Hung Sing (Jeong Yim line), Buck Sing, and the Chan-family King Mui line.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Long-and-short-range hybrid — swinging "windmill" hammer-fists (*sao choy, pao choy, kup choy*), wide circular arm strikes, strong waist torque, and a large weapons curriculum. Uniquely among southern arts, it keeps long-range mobility and big kicks.

**Training methodology.** Forms, sandbag and wooden-dummy striking, the famous "calling" vocal cues for each strike, two-person sets, extensive weapons (lion dance is a cultural staple).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: versatile range, powerful sweeping strikes, huge weapon/forms library, energetic and spectacular. Cons: wide swings can be telegraphed, forms-heavy, sparring culture varies widely by school.

**Integrates with.** Hung Gar, lion-dance/cultural wushu, Sanda.

## Praying Mantis (Tanglangquan, 螳螂拳)

**Root source & history.** Northern Praying Mantis is traditionally credited to **Wang Lang** of Shandong (C). Dating is genuinely contested: legends place Wang Lang anywhere from the Song to the late Ming/early Qing (~1650), and the "Wang Lang" name also belongs to an unrelated Eastern-Han figure — historians judge the *style* itself a Qing-era (18th–19th c.) creation retro-attributed to a folk hero (B/D). It supposedly fuses mantis arm-movements (Wang Lang watched a mantis defeat a cicada) with monkey footwork.

**Lineage.** Northern branches: Seven-Star (*Qi Xing*), Plum Blossom (*Mei Hua*), Six-Harmony (*Liu He*), Eight-Step (*Ba Bu*), Taiji Mantis. **Southern Praying Mantis** (Chow Gar, Chu Gar, Kwong Sai Jook Lum) is a *separate* Hakka art despite the shared name — a common point of confusion.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** "Mantis hook" hand (bent-wrist grabbing/trapping), continuous rapid trapping-and-striking chains, aggressive forward pressure, low monkey-like stepping in the northern lines; short explosive Hakka power in the southern lines.

**Training methodology.** Form-heavy with two-person trapping drills, hook-hand and forearm conditioning, footwork patterns.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: fast combination trapping, aggressive tempo, distinctive and effective hand-trapping concepts. Cons: forms-dominant, limited grappling/ground, sparring depth varies.

**Integrates with.** Northern Shaolin/Changquan, eagle claw, Sanda.

## Bak Mei / Pak Mei (白眉, "White Eyebrow")

**Root source & history.** Named for the legendary white-browed Taoist monk **Bak Mei**, cast in Shaolin folklore as one of the Five Elders — and, in the anti-Qing rebellion narrative, as a *traitor* who helped destroy Southern Shaolin (D). Documented transmission begins with **Cheung Lai-chuen** (1880–1964), who learned from Lin Sang/Lung Gemstone lines and popularized it in Guangdong/Hong Kong in the early 20th century (B).

**Lineage.** Parent: Hakka short-power family (shares roots with Southern Dragon and Southern Mantis). Branch: Cheung Lai-chuen line (the dominant modern lineage).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** "Phantom"/floating short power generated from the whole body (*ging*) — the six-power (*luk ging*) coordination of waist, spine, neck, jaw, shoulders, and *dim mak*-style pressure-point targeting. Pigeon-chest posture, sunk elbows, explosive sneeze-like short bursts. Core form: *Sup Ji Kuen* (Cross Pattern Fist).

**Training methodology.** Power-issuing (*fa ging*) drills, breathing coordination, short-bridge trapping. Famously close-guarded and "internal" in flavor despite being an external/southern art.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: devastating short-range explosive power, compact and economical, hard to read. Cons: secretive/scarce schools, near-zero competition exposure, effectiveness claims rarely pressure-tested publicly.

**Integrates with.** Southern Dragon, Southern Mantis, Wing Chun (shared close-range logic).

## Drunken Boxing (Zui Quan, 醉拳)

**Root source & history.** Not one school but a *method/genre* found inside many systems, imitating a drunkard's stagger. Two traditional roots: a **Buddhist/Shaolin** line (described as practicing "Eight Drunken Immortals boxing," a derivative of *ditangquan* ground-tumbling, in 18th-century manuals) (C), and a **Daoist** line themed on the mythic **Eight Immortals** (*ba xian*), each immortal an archetype/sub-style (D). Jackie Chan's *Drunken Master* (1978) cemented it in pop culture; the cinematic version is heavily stylized.

**Lineage.** Embedded within Shaolin, Northern Changquan, and various family styles as a specialized form rather than a freestanding lineage.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Deliberate off-balance swaying as deception; unpredictable angles; rolling, falling, and ground-tumbling (*ditang*) recoveries; strikes disguised as stumbles; using the body's "drunken" momentum to load power. Despite the name, it demands *exceptional* sobriety, balance, and core control.

**Training methodology.** Advanced — built on top of a solid base art. Heavy on falling/rolling conditioning, balance, and flexibility.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: genuine deception value, elite balance/body-control development, spectacular and fun. Cons: extremely hard to do well, high training-injury risk from falls, more demonstration than dependable combat method.

**Integrates with.** Shaolin/Changquan as a capstone form; *ditangquan* ground acrobatics.

## Modern Competitive Wushu (Taolu & Sanda)

**Root source & history.** A 20th-century state project, not an ancient art. Roots in the Republican-era **Central Guoshu Institute** (Nanjing, 1928), then systematized by the PRC from **1949** to standardize and sanitize traditional styles into sport (B). The **IWUF** (founded 1990) runs the biennial World Wushu Championships.

**Lineage.** Two clearly separate disciplines. **Taolu** (forms/performance): standardized Changquan, Nanquan, Taijiquan routines plus weapons, scored like gymnastics — modern Nanquan was *constructed* in the 1960s from southern styles. **Sanda / Sanshou** (full-contact): a military-derived kickboxing-plus-throws ruleset (punches, kicks, takedowns, *lei tai* platform throws; no ground game).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Taolu: aerial spins (540°/720° kicks), flexibility, *nandu* difficulty elements, speed and aesthetics. Sanda: boxing hands, low/round kicks, catch-and-throw counters, signature platform sweeps and slams.

**Training methodology.** Taolu = gymnastic athletic training and choreography. Sanda = pad work, sparring, takedown drilling — genuinely alive and combat-relevant.

**Pros & cons.** Taolu pros: athleticism, spectacle, accessibility, clear competition structure; cons: zero combat application, it is performance. Sanda pros: legitimately effective, MMA-transferable, real testing; cons: no ground fighting, fewer global gyms than boxing/MT.

**Integrates with.** Sanda ↔ MMA, kickboxing, Shuai Jiao; Taolu ↔ Shaolin demonstration.

## Tai Chi Chuan (Taijiquan, 太極拳) — the internal flagship

**Root source & history.** Best-documented lineage starts with **Chen Wangting** (1580–1660) of Chenjiagou (Chen Village), Henan, who synthesized family *quan*, qigong, and Daoist theory into Chen-style Taijiquan (B). The romantic claim that the Daoist immortal **Zhang Sanfeng** invented it on Wudang Mountain (Song/Yuan era) is legend/marketing (D). Yang Luchan (1799–1872) (B) learned the closely guarded art from Chen Changxing and brought it to Beijing, spawning the public styles.

**Lineage.** Chen (oldest) → **Yang** (Yang Luchan; by far the most-practiced worldwide) → **Wu/Hao** (Wu Yuxiang, 1812–1880; compact) → **Wu** (Quan You & Wu Jianquan, 1834–1942) → **Sun** (Sun Lutang, 1861–1932; fuses Xingyi + Bagua). The "24-form" Beijing simplified set (1956) is the global health standard.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Slow, continuous, spiraling *silk-reeling* (*chan si jin*) movement; rooting and structure; the *eight gates* (peng/lü/ji/an/cai/lie/zhou/kao) and five steps; **push hands** (*tui shou*) sensitivity training; *fa jin* explosive release (most visible in Chen). Whole-body relaxed power over muscular tension.

**Training methodology.** Solo form, standing post (*zhan zhuang*), push-hands. The split is stark: 95%+ of practitioners train it as health/meditation; a small minority train it as a fighting art with live push-hands and sparring.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: outstanding documented health benefits (balance, BP, fall prevention), lifelong/low-impact, deep internal skill, accessible everywhere. Cons: most schools are non-martial; "no-touch knockout" and invincibility claims are fraudulent (D) and were publicly humiliated when MMA's **Xu Xiaodong** beat self-styled tai chi "master" Wei Lei in under 20 seconds (2017), igniting the CMA-vs-reality debate (A).

**Integrates with.** Qigong, Bagua, Xingyi (the *neijia* trio); Sun style literally fuses all three.

## Baguazhang (八卦掌, "Eight Trigram Palm")

**Root source & history.** Created by **Dong Haichuan** (1797/1813–1882) (A/B), who began teaching it in Beijing in the 1860s, reportedly synthesizing a Daoist *circle-walking* meditative practice with his village martial background. Grouped with the Wudang "internal" school (the Wudang attribution itself is traditional framing, B/C).

**Lineage.** Founder Dong Haichuan → major branches by disciple: **Yin Fu** (small, precise), **Cheng Tinghua** (larger, dragon-flavored), plus Liang Zhenpu, Ma Gui, Song lines.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Constant **circle-walking** around an imagined opponent; continuous evasive footwork and body rotation; open-palm strikes, *kou bu / bai bu* twisting steps, throws and joint-locks delivered while circling. Mobility and angle-changing over linear force. Famous saying: a Bagua fighter is "never where the opponent strikes."

**Training methodology.** Endless circle-walking in low stance (the core engine of strength, leg endurance, and *qi* work), single/double palm-change forms, two-person rou shou.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: superb footwork and angles, strong vs multiple/encircling attackers in theory, excellent for evasion and joint manipulation, good health/leg conditioning. Cons: very long learning curve, scarce qualified teachers, sparse pressure-testing, demanding on knees.

**Integrates with.** Xingyi (classic complementary pairing — circular vs linear), Tai Chi, Sun-style Taiji, Shuai Jiao throws.

## Xingyiquan (形意拳, "Form-Intention Fist")

**Root source & history.** First historically real exponent: **Ji Longfeng** (Ji Jike) of Shanxi, late Ming (~early 1600s) (B), a renowned spearman who reputedly distilled spear method into empty-hand "Six Harmonies" (*Liuhe*). The popular attribution to Song-dynasty general **Yue Fei** is a retro-credit with no supporting evidence — a Chinese convention of crediting legends rather than oneself (D).

**Lineage.** Ji Longfeng → Shanxi, Hebei (most widespread; via Li Luoneng/Li Nengran), and Henan (Xinyi Liuhe / Muslim line) branches. Internal-arts pairing partner of Bagua and Taiji.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** The most aggressive and *linear* of the internal arts. **Five Elements** fists (*pi/zuan/beng/pao/heng* — splitting, drilling, crushing, pounding, crossing) mapped to the five-element cycle, plus the **Twelve Animals**. Whole-body simultaneous-step-and-strike (*san ti shi* "three-body" stance is the cornerstone), forward-driving intent, short explosive power.

**Training methodology.** Long holding of *san ti shi*, repetitive single-element drilling up and down the floor, then animal forms and two-person sets. Spartan, direct, repetition-driven.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: directly combative for an "internal" art, simple effective core, strong forward power and intent, good structure. Cons: austere and repetitive, limited lateral mobility (Bagua complements it), modest competition presence.

**Integrates with.** Baguazhang and Tai Chi (the *neijia* trinity), Sun-style Taiji, Sanda for live application.

## Qigong (氣功, "Energy Work")

**Root source & history.** Not a fighting art but the breath/energy-cultivation substrate beneath internal CMA and Shaolin conditioning. Roots in ancient *daoyin* gymnastics (the **Mawangdui silk *Daoyin Tu*** chart, ~168 BCE, is primary-document evidence) (A) and Daoist/Buddhist breathing. The Shaolin **Yijin Jing** ("Muscle/Tendon Change Classic") is a 17th-century qigong manual (B) — and the actual textual seed of the Bodhidharma myth. The term "qigong" as a unified label is a 20th-century coinage; the 1980s–90s "qigong fever" and the Falun Gong episode are documented modern phenomena (A/B).

**Lineage.** Two broad families: **medical/health qigong** (Eight Brocades / *Ba Duan Jin*, Six Healing Sounds, Wild Goose) and **martial/hard qigong** (Iron Shirt, Iron Palm body-conditioning).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Coordinated breath, posture, and attention to regulate body and (claimed) *qi* flow; standing post, slow stretching, visualization; hard-qigong conditioning that demonstrably toughens tissue.

**Training methodology.** Daily breathing/standing sets; hard styles add progressive impact conditioning.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: real, measurable benefits to balance, stress, breathing, and (in hard styles) tissue resilience; the engine room of internal-art power and Shaolin conditioning. Cons: external "emitted-qi" / no-touch healing and knockout claims are pseudoscientific (D); easily exploited by fraudulent "masters."

**Integrates with.** Tai Chi, Bagua, Xingyi, Hung Gar (Iron Wire), Shaolin conditioning.

## Jeet Kune Do (截拳道, "Way of the Intercepting Fist")

**Root source & history.** Founded by **Bruce Lee**, who coined the name in **1967** (he'd voiced the concept publicly in 1965 but resisted naming it) (A). The catalyst was his disavowal of rigid "classical" styles after his ~1964 challenge match with Wong Jack-man. Lee built outward from his **Wing Chun** base (under Ip Man) by adding **Western boxing** (footwork, the lead straight/jab), **fencing** (the intercepting stop-hit, lead-hand primacy, line-of-attack theory), and elements of *savate*, judo/jujutsu, and wrestling (A/B).

**Lineage.** Parent: Wing Chun + Western boxing + fencing (+ judo). Branches: **Jun Fan Gung Fu** / "Original JKD" (Lee's curriculum, preserved by Dan Inosanto, Taky Kimura, Ted Wong) vs **JKD Concepts** (Inosanto's open framework that folds in Filipino *kali*, *silat*, BJJ, and Muay Thai).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** "Style of no style": absorb what is useful, discard what is useless. Strong-side-forward stance, the intercepting **stop-hit**, the explosive lead straight punch, the low side/oblique kick (*jeet tek*), economy of motion, and the five "ways of attack." Philosophically the direct conceptual ancestor of **MMA**'s eclecticism.

**Training methodology.** Attribute training (speed, timing, distance), pad/focus-mitt work, trapping drills, sparring, and constant cross-pollination. Quality varies hugely between alive Inosanto-lineage gyms and rote "form-of-JKD" schools — an irony Lee explicitly warned against.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: pioneering hybrid philosophy, emphasis on aliveness and adaptability, real boxing/kicking tools, conceptually MMA-prescient. Cons: no fixed curriculum means wildly variable quality; pre-dates modern ground game (Lee died 1973, before BJJ's rise); often diluted by celebrity mythology.

**Integrates with.** Boxing, Muay Thai, BJJ, Filipino kali/silat, MMA — by design, it integrates with everything.
