# East Asian Striking — Karate, Taekwondo & Kickboxing

This cluster traces the percussive, stand-up striking arts of Okinawa, Japan, Korea, and China, plus the modern ring sports that fused them with boxing and Muay Thai. The spine of the story is a single diffusion arc: indigenous Okinawan *te* + southern Chinese boxing crystallized into **karate**, karate traveled to mainland Japan (1920s) and then globally, seeded **Korean** systems (taekwondo, Tang Soo Do) after 1945, and the full-contact impulse later spun off **kickboxing** in Japan (1966) and the United States (1974). Throughout, we separate measured/documentary history (A/B) from lineage oral tradition (C) and the romantic "ancient warrior" origin myths (D) that marketing keeps alive.

Provenance tiers are tagged inline: **(A)** measured/archaeological/primary-document, **(B)** scholarly consensus, **(C)** traditional/lineage oral history, **(D)** folklore/legend/marketing-myth.

## Karate (Okinawan roots & Japanese branches)

**Root source & history.** Karate descends from Okinawan *te* ("hand") — an indigenous grappling-and-striking practice — fused with southern Chinese quanfa (*toudi*, "Chinese/Tang hand") carried through the Ryukyu Kingdom's tribute trade with Fujian. By the 18th–19th centuries it had localized into three town-based currents named for Shuri, Naha, and Tomari (B). The popular claim that Okinawa's 1609 Satsuma weapons ban *caused* karate's empty-hand emphasis is a romantic myth — bans predated and postdated karate's development, and peasants did not secretly mass-train kata (D). The pivot to a documented public art is sharp: in **1901** Anko Itosu introduced a simplified karate into Okinawan school PE, sanitizing lethal techniques for children (A), and in **1905** the name shifted from "Tang hand" toward "empty hand" (*kara-te*) (B).

**Lineage (parents → branches).**
- **Shuri-te** (royal capital, fast linear power; Sokon Matsumura → Anko Itosu) → **Shorin-ryu** family → **Shotokan**, and heavily into **Shito-ryu**, **Wado-ryu**, **Korean kwans** (B/C).
- **Naha-te** (port city, breathing/close-range, White Crane influence; Kanryo Higaonna) → **Goju-ryu** (B/C).
- **Tomari-te** (harbor town, hybrid footwork) → folded into Shorin lineages and Shito-ryu (C).

### Shotokan
**History/lineage.** Founded by Gichin Funakoshi (1868–1957), the Okinawan schoolteacher who demonstrated karate at the Japanese Ministry of Education exhibition in **1922** and at Jigoro Kano's Kodokan, then stayed in Tokyo (A/B). His first permanent dojo, the **Shotokan** ("Shoto's hall," from his pen-name Shoto), opened **1936**; students formed the **Japan Karate Association (JKA)** in **1949**, later led technically by Masatoshi Nakayama (A). Parent: Shuri-te/Shorin-ryu. Branches: JKA, Shotokai, SKIF, and a huge global diaspora; it is also the technical taproot of early Korean taekwondo and Tang Soo Do.
**Mechanics.** Long, deep stances (zenkutsu-dachi, kiba-dachi), hip-driven reverse punch (gyaku-zuki), strong front/side kicks, linear in-and-out timing; one-strike-finish (*ikken hissatsu*) ethos.
**Training.** Kihon (basics by repetition), kata (Heian → Tekki → Bassai/Kanku), and kumite from three-step (sanbon) to free sparring; light-to-medium contact, point-style.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: huge availability, excellent fundamentals, explosive power. Cons: deep stances and point-sparring transfer imperfectly to live fighting; limited clinch/ground.
**Integrates with.** Boxing (hand range), kickboxing, judo/BJJ (to cover the ground gap).

### Goju-ryu
**History/lineage.** Codified by Chojun Miyagi (1888–1953) from Naha-te under Kanryo Higaonna; the name "Goju" ("hard-soft") was adopted and the style recognized by the Dai Nippon Butoku Kai in **1933** (A/B). Branches: Okinawan Goju, Japanese Goju (Yamaguchi "The Cat"), and it is a parent of Kyokushin and Isshin-ryu.
**Mechanics.** Close-range infighting, circular blocks, Sanchin breathing/body conditioning, short hooking strikes, hand traps, low kicks; signature kata *Sanchin* and *Tensho*.
**Training.** Hojo undo (stone/jar tool conditioning), Sanchin tension breathing, kata-bunkai application, body toughening.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: practical close range, strong body mechanics and conditioning. Cons: isometric Sanchin loading debated for spinal/blood-pressure risk; less footwork emphasis.
**Integrates with.** Kyokushin, clinch arts, Muay Thai (low kicks/clinch).

### Shito-ryu
**History/lineage.** Created **1928–1934** by Kenwa Mabuni (1889–1952), who studied both Shuri-te (Itosu) and Naha-te (Higaonna) — the name "Shito" honors both teachers' characters (A/B). Branches: Shitokai, Shukokai, Hayashi-ha, Itosu-kai.
**Mechanics.** Largest kata syllabus of any major style (50+), blending Shuri speed with Naha power; balanced linear/circular.
**Training.** Kata-centric with extensive bunkai; competition kata is a Shito-ryu/Shotokan stronghold.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: technical breadth, strong sport-kata pedigree. Cons: kata volume can dilute live-fighting focus.
**Integrates with.** Shotokan, sport kumite, kobudo.

### Wado-ryu
**History/lineage.** Founded **1934/1939** by Hironori Otsuka (1892–1982), a Funakoshi student who also held menkyo in Shindo Yoshin-ryu jujutsu — uniquely karate + Japanese jujutsu (B). "Wado" = "way of harmony."
**Mechanics.** Body-shifting (*taisabaki*) and evasion over hard blocking; shorter, more natural stances; integral tai-sabaki and joint/throw concepts.
**Training.** Kata plus paired yakusoku kumite drills emphasizing evasion and timing.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: mobile, efficient, less attritional on the body. Cons: lighter power emphasis; jujutsu component often underdeveloped in sport dojos.
**Integrates with.** Jujutsu, judo, point-fighting.

### Kyokushin
**History/lineage.** Formalized **1964** by Masutatsu (Mas) Oyama (1923–1994), Korean-Japanese, drawing on Goju-ryu and Shotokan (B). The original full-contact, knockdown karate; parent of Seidokaikan, Ashihara, Enshin, and the K-1 promotional lineage.
**Mechanics.** Bare-knuckle full-contact (no head punches, but full-power body punches and head kicks), heavy low kicks, forward pressure, "absorb-and-advance."
**Training.** Hard sparring, body conditioning, the 100-man kumite test, tameshiwari (breaking).
**Pros/cons.** Pros: genuine toughness, tested under contact, great conditioning. Cons: no-hands-to-head rule warps defense; high training-injury load.
**Integrates with.** Kickboxing/K-1, Muay Thai, MMA striking.

### Uechi-ryu
**History/lineage.** Brought from Fujian by Kanbun Uechi (1877–1948), who studied Pangai-noon (half-hard, half-soft) at Fuzhou **1897–1910**, taught in Wakayama, Japan, and renamed it **Uechi-ryu Karate-jutsu in 1940** (A/C). Among the most directly Chinese-derived karate.
**Mechanics.** Three core kata only originally (Sanchin, Seisan, Sanseirui), one-knuckle and spearhand strikes, toe kicks, crane/tiger circularity, heavy body conditioning.
**Training.** Sanchin conditioning, partner pounding (kotekitae), kata application.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: efficient short syllabus, strong conditioning, pragmatic close range. Cons: pointed-weapon strikes (toe/knuckle) need conditioned tools; small school footprint.
**Integrates with.** Goju-ryu, southern kung fu, clinch work.

### Isshin-ryu
**History/lineage.** Synthesized **1947–48**, formally named **January 1956** by Tatsuo Shimabuku (1908–1975) from Shorin-ryu + Goju-ryu + kobudo (A/B). Spread widely via U.S. Marines stationed on Okinawa.
**Mechanics.** Vertical-fist punch (thumb on top), shorter natural stances, snapping kicks, integrated weapons (bo, sai, tonfa).
**Training.** 8 empty-hand + 7 weapon kata; practical, streamlined curriculum.
**Pros/cons.** Pros: efficient, weapons-integrated, strong U.S. presence. Cons: smaller/fragmented federations; quality varies.
**Integrates with.** Kobudo, other Okinawan styles, self-defense systems.

## Taekwondo

**Root source & history.** Taekwondo is a **mid-20th-century Korean synthesis**, not an ancient art. After the 1945 liberation, Koreans who had trained Japanese **Shotokan/Shudokan karate** during the occupation opened schools (**kwan**) in 1944–1946; Shotokan is the dominant technical parent, with some indigenous **Taekkyeon** and **subak/gwonbeop** flavor and Chinese influence (B). The name *taekwon-do* was coined/advocated by **Gen. Choi Hong-hi in 1955–57**. Claims of an unbroken "2,000-year-old Korean martial art" tracing to Goguryeo tomb murals and Hwarang warriors are nationalist marketing-myth — the murals show generic combat, not modern TKD (D). The **Korea Taekwondo Association** unified the kwans in **1959/1965**.

**Lineage (parents → branches).** Parents: Shotokan karate (primary), Taekkyeon (minor). The **nine kwans** (Chung Do Kwan, Moo Duk Kwan, Song Moo Kwan, Jidokwan, Chang Moo Kwan, etc.) → two modern poles:
- **ITF** — International Taekwon-Do Federation, founded **1966** by Gen. Choi Hong-hi; after Choi moved the ITF toward North Korea (he relocated to Canada then aligned with the DPRK in the 1970s–80s), it fragmented into multiple ITFs after his 2002 death.
- **WT (Kukkiwon)** — Kukkiwon (World TKD HQ) established **1972**; the **WTF founded 1973** (renamed **World Taekwondo, June 2017**, to ditch the "WTF" acronym). This is the **Olympic** branch (demo 1988, full medal sport from **2000**).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Kick-dominant: fast roundhouse, side, axe, hook, and spinning/jumping spin kicks; long-range, bouncing footwork. ITF uses the "sine-wave" power generation and continuous-contact sparring with hand techniques scored; WT uses electronic-scoring (PSS) hogu sparring that rewards rapid high kicks and de-emphasizes punching to the head.

**Training methodology.** Patterns (ITF *tul* / WT *poomsae* / Kukkiwon Taegeuk), kicking drills on paddles, step-sparring, breaking (*kyukpa*), and rank-belt progression. WT competition is heavily point-tactical; ITF retains more hand striking.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: world-class kicking speed/variety, athletic, Olympic pathway, extremely accessible (schools everywhere, kid-friendly). Cons: WT rules breed a sport bias (hands low, back to opponent, light "tag" kicks) with weak hands and no clinch/ground; "ancient lineage" branding inflates self-defense expectations.

**Integrates with.** Boxing/kickboxing (to fix hands), Muay Thai, BJJ; many MMA strikers raid TKD for spinning/oblique kicks (Anderson Silva, Anthony Pettis).

## Tang Soo Do

**Root source & history.** Founded by **Hwang Kee**, who opened the **Moo Duk Kwan on 9 November 1945** (A). Originally "Hwa Soo Do," renamed **Tang Soo Do** ("way of the Tang/China hand," i.e., the Korean reading of the same characters as *karate*'s older name); later branches use **Soo Bahk Do** (B). Technically it is **Shotokan-flavored karate** plus Hwang Kee's study of the Chinese *Tang* texts and some Taekkyeon (B); the romanticized derivation purely from ancient *subak* is overstated (D/C).

**Lineage.** Parent: Shuri-te → Shotokan (shared kwan-era root with taekwondo). Branches: Moo Duk Kwan Tang Soo Do, Soo Bahk Do, and many American Tang Soo Do federations (popularized in the U.S. partly via Chuck Norris).

**Mechanics & training.** Karate-like linear strikes and stances with TKD-style high/spinning kicks; *hyung* forms (Pyung Ahn = Pinan/Heian, Bassai), one-step sparring, breaking. Distinctive midnight-blue belt for masters in Moo Duk Kwan.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: balanced hand-and-foot striking, strong forms tradition, classical feel. Cons: politically/organizationally fragmented; mostly point/semi-contact, limited live pressure-testing; clinch/ground absent.

**Integrates with.** Taekwondo, Shotokan, hapkido (for joint locks/self-defense rounding).

## Hapkido (striking side)

**Root source & history.** Founded by **Choi Yong-sool** (1904–1986), who claimed to have learned **Daito-ryu Aiki-jujutsu under Takeda Sokaku** during ~30 years in Japan — a lineage **disputed and unsupported by Takeda's surviving enrollment/fee records** (C, claim flagged; the dispute itself is B). Returning to Korea after WWII, Choi's art (Yu Kwon Sool / "Hapki") was expanded by students — above all **Ji Han-jae** (b. 1936) — who added the **dynamic kicking and striking** (from Taekkyeon-influenced kicking and contemporary Korean striking) that gives modern hapkido its percussive side. First formal school **1951**; name "Hapkido" first used **1958** (A/B).

**Lineage.** Parents: Daito-ryu aiki-jujutsu (claimed) + Korean kicking/striking + judo throws/ground. Branches: Sin Moo Hapkido (Ji Han-jae), Combat Hapkido, Hankido, and many eclectic offshoots.

**Mechanics & training.** A hybrid: circular joint locks, throws, and pressure points (the aiki side) plus **spinning, jumping, and low-line kicks and palm/fist strikes** (the striking side). Emphasis on redirecting force, flowing combinations, and weapons (cane, belt, knife defense). Training is technique-and-drill heavy with less full-contact sparring than ring arts.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: broad self-defense toolkit (strikes + locks + throws), dynamic kicks, weapon defenses. Cons: curriculum sprawl and uneven quality; many techniques are compliance-drilled rather than pressure-tested; founder lineage is contested marketing (D-adjacent).

**Integrates with.** Taekwondo (kicks), judo/jujutsu (locks/throws), Tang Soo Do.

## American Kickboxing (Full-Contact Karate)

**Root source & history.** Born in the **early-to-mid 1970s** when U.S. point-karate competitors wanted to settle fights with knockouts. The **Professional Karate Association (PKA)** was formed **1974** by Don and Judy Quine with Mike Anderson, holding its first world championships at the Los Angeles Sports Arena (telecast on ABC) (A). Early champions: **Joe Lewis, Bill "Superfoot" Wallace, Jeff Smith, Benny Urquidez** (A).

**Lineage.** Parents: American sport karate (Shotokan/Tang Soo Do/Kenpo lineages) + Western **boxing**. Sanctioning branches: PKA → KICK, ISKA, WKA, IKF. Distinguished from later "international rules" by banning leg kicks.

**Mechanics & training.** "Above-the-waist" rules: boxing-style hand combinations plus karate/TKD kicks to the head and torso, **no low kicks, no clinch, no knees** (classic PKA). Padded gloves, foot pads, full power, a minimum number of kicks per round mandated. Boxing-gym methodology: bag/pad work, sparring, roadwork, conditioning.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: hard, honest contact; great cardio and hand-foot integration; legit competition history. Cons: no-leg-kick ruleset is tactically narrow and outdated vs. Muay Thai/Dutch rules; no clinch/ground.

**Integrates with.** Boxing, Muay Thai (to add low kicks/clinch), MMA striking.

## Japanese Kickboxing / K-1

**Root source & history.** Coined by promoter **Osamu Noguchi**, who with karateka **Tatsuo Yamada** built a karate-vs-Muay-Thai hybrid; the **first event was held in Osaka on 11 April 1966** and the Kickboxing Association formed the same year (A/B). It boomed on Japanese TV (three channels by 1970), faded, then was reborn when **Kazuyoshi Ishii (Seidokaikan karate) founded K-1 in 1993** — a heavyweight, glove-and-shorts, stand-up tournament fusing kickboxing, Muay Thai, Kyokushin, and Western boxing (A/B).

**Lineage.** Parents: Muay Thai + Kyokushin/Japanese karate + boxing. Branches: K-1, Shootboxing (adds standing submissions), RISE, Krush, and the broader "Japanese/Oji rules" lineage feeding into Glory.

**Mechanics & training.** Punches, kicks (including **low kicks**, the key difference from American rules), knees; **K-1 rules limit clinch/knee duration** vs. full Muay Thai. Heavy pad/bag work, hard sparring, tournament-in-a-night conditioning.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: complete stand-up striking, spectacular KO-driven format, elite competition depth. Cons: limited clinch (vs. Muay Thai) and no ground; demanding, injury-heavy training.

**Integrates with.** Muay Thai, boxing, Kyokushin, MMA striking.

## Sanda / Sanshou (Chinese Kickboxing)

**Root source & history.** The full-contact **combat half of modern wushu**. The PLA developed a streamlined close-combat *sanshou* ("free hand") in the **1950s** from Shuai Jiao wrestling, traditional striking, boxing, and judo; the **General Administration of Sport of China expanded it into a civilian sport from 1979**, debuting at the 1979 National Games (A/B). Renamed **Sanda** in common use. It was explicitly designed to give Chinese traditional martial arts a tested sparring platform.

**Lineage.** Parents: traditional Chinese kung fu striking + **Shuai Jiao** (Chinese wrestling) + boxing/judo. Branches: amateur Sanda (protective gear), professional Sanda (lighter gear), and military/lethal sanshou (elbows, knees, locks barred in sport).

**Mechanics & training.** Boxing-and-kicking **plus catches, sweeps, takedowns, and throws** — its signature is catching a kick and dumping the opponent, and throws off the *leitai* (raised platform); sport rules bar elbows, ground fighting, and strikes to spine/groin. Training blends kung fu basics with modern pad/wrestling/sparring methodology.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: genuinely complete stand-up (strikes + takedowns/throws), pragmatic, gives kung fu a live-testing outlet. Cons: limited outside China; no ground game; quality depends on whether a school spars hard or stays forms-focused.

**Integrates with.** Shuai Jiao, kickboxing/Muay Thai, MMA (the kick-catch-to-throw transfers well).
