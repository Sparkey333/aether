# Video-Game Fighting Styles, Grounded in Reality — MGS CQC & Beyond

Fighting games and action titles invent "styles," but the best ones are scaffolded on real arts — some with credited masters on the mocap stage, others borrowing a name and little else. This section traces each fictional system back to its real roots, names who designed or consulted on it (with provenance tiers), and rates how grounded it actually is via `realismIfFictional`. Provenance tiers: **(A)** developer/primary & credited consultants; **(B)** journalistic/analytic consensus; **(C)** fan-community attribution; **(D)** speculation/myth.

---

## Metal Gear Solid — CQC (Close Quarters Combat)

**In-game style.** Hideo Kojima's signature melee system, introduced in *MGS3: Snake Eater* (2004) and refined through *MGS4* (2008), *Peace Walker* (2010), and *MGSV: The Phantom Pain* (2015). CQC is a seamless flow of grabs, throws, joint locks, chokes, a knife (the CQC fighting knife), and the defining "handgun-in-the-grapple" — pinning an enemy as a human shield or interrogation hostage while keeping a pistol oriented downrange. It is built for a lone operator who must transition instantly between non-lethal control, lethal finish, and shooting.

**Real roots.** CQC is a genuine military-combatives concept, not a named civilian art. Its DNA is **WWII Allied close-combat doctrine** — the Fairbairn–Sykes "Defendu" and W.E. Fairbairn's *Get Tough!*, plus Rex Applegate's *Kill or Get Killed* (B) — which themselves distilled **judo** and **jujutsu** into brutal, gross-motor edged-and-empty-hand techniques for soldiers. The throws, wrist controls, and chokes read as **judo/aikido/jiu-jitsu** (B); the integrated draw-and-shoot-from-the-clinch maps to modern **point-shooting / weapon-retention** taught to police and special forces (B). In lore, CQC is credited to **The Boss**, who taught it to **Naked Snake (Big Boss)** — fictional provenance (D) layered over the real combatives base.

**Who designed/consulted.** **Motosada Mori**, the series' long-running military advisor and a former SWAT instructor with 20+ years' experience, adapted the "handgun" CQC method for Snake and performed the **motion capture for the CQC sequences himself** in *MGS3* (2004) (A). Mori advised across the series from the first *MGS* (1998) through *MGSV* (2015) (A). Kojima Productions designed the in-game system around Mori's demonstrations (A).

**How realistic, really.** The *components* are real and the *consultant pedigree is legitimate* — that's rare. The unrealistic part is the **cinematic density**: real soldiers do not chain effortless one-armed throws into hostage-pins against multiple aware opponents, and holding a struggling man as a shield while accurately pistol-shooting is far harder than the game implies. Treat CQC as authentic ingredients arranged for spectacle.

**Train to emulate.** **Judo** + **Brazilian jiu-jitsu** for the throws/control, a **military combatives / Krav Maga** program for the weapon-retention and brutal efficiency, and **defensive-handgun (retention shooting)** training for the pistol-in-clinch piece.

## Sifu — Pak Mei (Bak Mei) Kung Fu

**In-game style.** Sloclap's 2022 brawler renders a single coherent close-range kung fu: explosive short-power strikes, phoenix-eye fists, parries, structure-breaking, and improvised weapons (staff, bat, machete).

**Real roots.** **Pak Mei (Bak Mei / "White Eyebrow")** — a southern Chinese close-range system famous for short bridge power, sinking root, and fast hand traps (A/B).

**Who designed/consulted.** **Benjamin Colussi**, a Pak Mei master and founder of the Lao Wei San – Pak Mei school in Paris, served as fight choreographer and consultant; he created **213 moves** for the protagonist via keyframe and motion capture, plus struggle mechanics and NPC behavior (A).

**How realistic, really.** Move-for-move the choreography is unusually authentic — the postures, line of force, and short-power generation are recognizably Pak Mei. The fantasy is the **arcade durability** (taking dozens of hits, the aging/revive mechanic), not the technique.

**Train to emulate.** **Pak Mei / Bak Mei kung fu**, or its cousin **Southern Praying Mantis / Lung Ying (Dragon)** for similar short-bridge close combat.

## Sleeping Dogs — Wing Chun / JKD-Flavored Brawling

**In-game style.** Wei Shen's Hong Kong street combat: trapping, fast chain-punches, environmental finishers, counters off blocks, plus flashier kicks and joint locks.

**Real roots.** **Wing Chun** (defensive inside-fighting, chain punches) blended with **Jeet Kune Do** (Bruce Lee's range-and-economy ideas, joint locks, kicks), with judo/aikido/Muay-Thai accents (B). The Hong Kong setting deliberately invokes Ip Man and Bruce Lee.

**Who designed/consulted.** United Front Games built the combat as a Batman-*Arkham*-style counter system skinned in HK cinema kung fu; specific named master credits are thin, so the art attribution sits at journalistic/community consensus (B/C).

**How realistic, really.** The *vocabulary* (trapping, chain punches, wrist locks) is real Wing Chun/JKD, but it's packaged as flowy environmental cinema. Mid realism: recognizable arts, exaggerated delivery.

**Train to emulate.** **Wing Chun** for the trapping/chain-punch core, then **JKD** for range and adaptability.

## Tekken — Per-Character Real Styles

**In-game style.** A roster where most fighters carry a *named real art* rather than a generic "fighting game" moveset.

**Real roots (selected).**
- **Mishima-ryū Karate** (Heihachi/Kazuya/Jin's base) — derived from **Shotokan karate** (B).
- **King** — **lucha libre** (later more shoot-wrestling flavored), a genuine catalog of luchador throws and suplexes (B).
- **Hwoarang** — **Taekwondo**, heavily kick-based (A/B).
- **Craig Marduk** — **vale tudo** ground-and-pound (B).
- **Paul Phoenix** — **judo / "integrated martial arts"** (B).
- Others: **Marshall/Forest Law** (JKD homage), **Baek** (TKD), **Asuka/Jin** (Kazama traditional), **Lei** (Five Animals kung fu).

**Who designed/consulted.** Namco's design team (Katsuhiro Harada's lineage) researched and animated real styles per character; attribution is studio-stated for some, journalistic for most (A/B).

**How realistic, really.** The *style identities* are real and the moves are often recognizable (lucha suplexes, TKD spin kicks), but Tekken layers fireball-tier supers, infinite juggles, and Mishima electrics on top. Medium realism — real skeleton, fantasy muscle.

**Train to emulate.** Pick the character's named art: **Shotokan** (Mishima), **lucha/amateur wrestling** (King), **Taekwondo** (Hwoarang), **MMA/vale tudo** (Marduk).

## Virtua Fighter — The "Most Technically Accurate" Fighter

**In-game style.** Sega's 1993 3D pioneer, widely cited as the most martial-arts-accurate fighting game: every character maps to a real, often obscure, traditional style with realistic knockdown physics.

**Real roots (selected).**
- **Akira Yuki** — **Bājíquán (Hakkyoku-ken)**, a real explosive short-range Chinese art (A/B).
- **Pai Chan** — **Mízōngquán (Ensei-ken)** (B).
- **Lau Chan** — **Tiger/Swallow fist, Huāquán-family (Kōen-ken)** (B).
- **Vanessa Lewis** — **Vale Tudo / Muay Thai** stances (B).
- Others: **Wolf** (pro wrestling), **Jeffry** (Pancratium/grappling), **Sarah/Jacky** (Jeet Kune Do), **Lion** (Tōrōken / praying mantis), **Aoi** (Aiki-jūjutsu).

**Who designed/consulted.** Yu Suzuki's AM2 team built the game around real-style research and a then-novel physics engine where hits produced realistic falls (A/B).

**How realistic, really.** The *highest* of the fighting-game pack: stance, footwork, and the obscure-but-real style choices (Bajiquan, Mizongquan) reflect genuine study, and the physics resist the floatiness of rivals. Still a game — no chip damage in real Bajiquan — but the foundation is unusually honest.

**Train to emulate.** **Bājíquán** for Akira; broadly, the real art behind your main (e.g., **Aiki-jūjutsu** for Aoi, **MMA** for Vanessa).

## Dead or Alive — Real Styles, Loosely Held

**In-game style.** Team Ninja's fast counter-hold-based fighter where each character nominally practices a real art.

**Real roots (selected).** **Jann Lee** — **Jeet Kune Do** (Bruce Lee homage); **Leifang** — **Tàijí / Taikyoku-ken**; **Helena** — **Piguaquan**; **Gen Fu** — **Xinyi Liuhe**; **Bass** — **pro wrestling**; ninja characters — **Tenjin Mon ninjutsu (fictional clan)** (B).

**Who designed/consulted.** Creator **Tomonobu Itagaki** chose real styles but stated openly that he prized *playable fun over authenticity* — he redesigned the Piguaquan character "from the ground up," taking liberties (A). So the art names are real; fidelity is intentionally loose.

**How realistic, really.** Lower than VF or Sifu — the labels are real but the execution is stylized for the hold-counter triangle and flashy juggles. The Piguaquan and Taiji *flavor* survives; the rigor does not.

**Train to emulate.** **Jeet Kune Do** (Jann Lee), **Piguaquan** or **Bajiquan** (Helena), **Tai chi push-hands** (Leifang).

## Absolver — Composite Original Combat Style

**In-game style.** Sloclap's 2017 online martial-arts RPG built around a "combat deck" — players chain attacks across four stances, *learning* enemy moves by being hit. Distinct in-game styles: Forsaken (parry), Khalt (absorb), Windfall (dodge), Stagger (drunken).

**Real roots.** A *deliberate fusion* rather than one art: the team (ex-Ubisoft devs who later made Sifu) drew on multiple striking and grappling traditions, framed as "combat is a dance." The Stagger style is recognizably **Zui Quan (drunken boxing)**; the flow-and-stance logic echoes **kung fu** and **Capoeira**-like movement (B). Mocap used real martial artists (A/B).

**How realistic, really.** Conceptually grounded — stance transitions, range management, and reading an opponent are real combat principles — but the styles are invented composites, and the "learn moves by getting hit" loop is pure game design. Medium realism in *principle*, fictional in *specifics*.

**Train to emulate.** A broad striking base — **kung fu (Sanda)** or **kickboxing** — plus **Zui Quan** flavor for Stagger; the deck-building mindset maps to **MMA** style-mixing.

## The Last of Us — Improvised Survival Violence

**In-game style.** Naughty Dog's *TLOU* (2013) and *Part II* (2020) melee is not a "style" but desperate, grounded survival violence: shivs, pipes, bricks, bottles, choke takedowns, and grappling that prioritizes ending fights fast with whatever's at hand.

**Real roots.** Improvised-weapon and **survival/street self-defense** logic — the antithesis of sport martial arts. The design goal was *visceral, risky, resource-scarce* combat (A): stealth takedowns alternating with brutal close brawls because ammo is rare. No single art; the throat-grab finishers and weapon-of-opportunity use echo real **combatives** principles (use environment, gross motor, immediate incapacitation).

**Who designed/consulted.** Naughty Dog iterated melee prototypes for "viscerality and risk," with extensive motion capture of the actors (Troy Baker, Ashley Johnson) (A). Combat realism here is *situational*, not style-credentialed.

**How realistic, really.** Paradoxically high in *spirit* — real violence is ugly, improvised, and over fast, which TLOU captures better than most "martial arts" games — but Joel/Ellie/Abby absorb damage no human would. Realistic *philosophy*, gamey *durability*.

**Train to emulate.** **Krav Maga** or a reputable **self-defense/combatives** course emphasizing environmental weapons and escape; **wrestling** for the takedown control.

## Splinter Cell — Sam Fisher's Krav Maga

**In-game style.** Sam Fisher's lethal, efficient close combat, foregrounded from *Conviction* (2010) onward: fast disarms, neck-breaks, brutal takedowns from concealment.

**Real roots.** **Krav Maga** — the Israeli Defense Forces' "close combat" system, itself a pragmatic blend of **boxing, judo, aikido, and wrestling** with no sport rules (B). *Blacklist* (2013) lore expands Fisher's skills to **Systema, Filipino Kali, and jiu-jitsu** (B/C).

**Who designed/consulted.** Animation director **Gilles Monteil** stated in a *Conviction* dev diary that the team chose Krav Maga because they needed something "quick, efficient and brutal — something without any rules" (A). In-fiction, Fisher trained Krav Maga to a high civilian rank (D, lore).

**How realistic, really.** Strong style-fit: Krav Maga *is* the doctrine a real black-ops operative would use, and the disarms/retention are conceptually sound. The instant lethal neck-snaps are Hollywood; real Krav favors gross-motor strikes and escape over cinematic kills. Good realism in intent.

**Train to emulate.** **Krav Maga**, supplemented by **Filipino Kali** (knife/disarm) and **Systema** (relaxed movement) to mirror Blacklist-era Fisher.

## Yakuza / Like a Dragon — Kiryu's Brawling Styles

**In-game style.** Kazuma Kiryu's switchable styles (*Yakuza 0*): **Brawler**, **Rush** (boxing), **Beast** (grappling/environmental), and the canonical **Dragon of Dojima** — plus heat-action finishers using bikes, signs, and tables.

**Real roots.** A practical blend: **street brawling + Kyokushin-flavored karate + judo throws + pro wrestling** (B). Analysts note Kiryu's hook-heavy combos with high kicks mirror **Kyokushin** (which bans face punches, pushing kicks high), and his judo throws echo Kyokushin founder **Mas Oyama**'s judo black-belt background (B). Rush style is essentially **Western boxing** footwork.

**How realistic, really.** Medium-high *base*: the punches, hooks, low kicks, and throws are real and well-animated; the **heat actions** (suplexing a man through a guardrail, ten-foot uppercuts) are pure spectacle. Strip the finishers and Kiryu is a believable bouncer-brawler.

**Train to emulate.** **Kyokushin karate** (the hook-and-high-kick core), **boxing** for Rush, **judo** for the throws.

## For Honor — HEMA-Inspired Weapon Styles

**In-game style.** Ubisoft's 2017 weapons fighter pits **Knights, Vikings, and Samurai** in a directional "Art of Battle" system: stance-based guard on three planes, with longswords, axes, katana, nodachi, spears, and shields.

**Real roots.** **HEMA (Historical European Martial Arts)** for the knights — German longsword *guards* (the three-direction guard maps loosely to real *Vom Tag / Ochs / Pflug*-style high/side stances) (B); **kenjutsu** vocabulary for the samurai; period-flavored axe/shield work for the Vikings (B).

**Who designed/consulted.** Creative Director **Jason Vandenberghe** trained **German longsword at a local dojo**, which shaped the directional combat concept (A). Mocap used actors trained for weeks by **stunt coordinator Marc Désourdy** under animation director **Khai Nguyen** (A). It's stunt-and-research-grounded rather than HEMA-federation-certified.

**How realistic, really.** The *directional-guard core* is a clever abstraction of a real HEMA truth (you defend the line the attack comes on), and weapon reach/timing feel plausible. But the durability (surviving many sword blows), faction-blending, and flashy executions are fantasy. Solid *conceptual* realism, low *physical* fidelity (one real longsword cut ends a fight).

**Train to emulate.** **HEMA longsword** (Liechtenauer tradition) for knights; **kenjutsu / kendo** for samurai; **sword-and-board / Viking reenactment combat** for the Norse styles.
