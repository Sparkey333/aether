# Western & European Arts — Boxing, Savate, HEMA, Fencing

This cluster spans the Western striking, fencing, and grappling lineages: the bare-knuckle prize ring that became modern **boxing**, the French foot-fighting tradition of **Savate**, the manuscript-reconstructed weapon and wrestling systems of **HEMA**, the Olympic blade sports, and the Victorian/military eclectic-defense bridge running from **Bartitsu** to **Defendu**. Two threads run through everything below. First, **boxing is among the most pressure-tested striking arts on Earth** — three centuries of full-contact competition have stress-selected its mechanics harder than almost any other unarmed system. Second, HEMA is a genuine *reconstruction* discipline: its techniques are recovered from surviving fightbooks (*Fechtbücher*), so where the JSON tiers normally read (A) for primary evidence, HEMA's source-documents are (A) but the living technique built atop them is honestly (B).

---

## Boxing (English Pugilism → Queensberry → Modern)

**Root source & history.** Fist-fighting for sport is ancient (Greek *pygmachia*, Roman *pugilatus*), but the unbroken Western lineage begins in early-18th-century London. **James Figg** opened his amphitheatre and is styled the first English champion / "father of modern boxing" c. 1719 (C). **Jack Broughton** codified the first rule set — **Broughton's Rules, 1743** (A, dated document) — banning hitting a downed man and below-the-belt blows after a fatality in his ring. These hardened into the **London Prize Ring Rules (1838, revised 1853)** (A), which kept bare fists but permitted throws and grappling. The decisive break is the **Marquess of Queensberry Rules**: drafted by **John Graham Chambers** in 1865 and published **1867** under the patronage of **John Douglas, 9th Marquess of Queensberry** (A) — mandating padded gloves, three-minute rounds, the ten-count, and no wrestling. This created the modern sport.

**Lineage.** Greek/Roman pugilism (ancestral) → English bare-knuckle pugilism (Figg, Broughton) → London Prize Ring → **Queensberry gloved boxing** → branches into amateur/Olympic boxing, professional boxing, and as a striking module feeding **kickboxing, MMA, and modern combatives**.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Four punches (jab, cross, hook, uppercut) off a bladed stance; head movement (slip, bob-and-weave, roll), footwork (pivot, cut-off), guard structures (high/peek-a-boo, Philly shell, cross-arm). Signature concepts: distance management behind the jab, the 1-2, body-head combination work, the counter-puncher's "pull counter," and ring generalship. Hands only, waist-up — a deliberately narrow toolset drilled to extraordinary depth.

**Training methodology.** Roadwork, heavy bag, double-end bag, speed bag, focus mitts, and the defining element: **graduated live sparring**. The narrow ruleset makes hard sparring relatively survivable, which is precisely why the techniques are so battle-tested.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: elite hand striking, footwork, conditioning, and defense; cheap and ubiquitous; ferocious 1v1 striking. Cons: no kicks, clinch, takedowns, or weapons; vulnerable to leg/clinch/grappling fighters; repeated-head-trauma (CTE) risk is real over a career.

**Integrates with.** Muay Thai, Dutch kickboxing, MMA, Savate, BJJ (as the striking base), and military combatives.

## Bare-Knuckle Boxing (Prize Ring → BKFC Revival)

**Root source & history.** This is boxing's *ancestor* rather than a derivative — the London Prize Ring era of **John L. Sullivan**, whose 1889 bout vs. Jake Kilrain is often cited as the last great American bare-knuckle heavyweight title fight under London rules (B/C). Gloved Queensberry boxing then displaced it. The modern revival is the **Bare Knuckle Fighting Championship (BKFC)**, founded **April 2018** by **David Feldman**, whose **BKFC 1** ran **2 June 2018 in Cheyenne, Wyoming** (A) — the first state-sanctioned bare-knuckle event in the U.S. since the 19th century.

**Lineage.** English/American prize ring (Broughton → London Prize Ring) → dormant/underground 20th century → **BKFC and modern promotions** (BYB Extreme, Gamebred). Distinct from Russian/Irish folk bare-knuckle traditions that persisted regionally.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Closer range and a more squared, shorter stance than gloved boxing; emphasis on the "toe-line" engagement, defensive hand-fighting, and shorter, snappier punches. Bare fists cut faster but break easier, shifting strategy toward accuracy over volume.

**Training methodology.** Largely a boxing curriculum with conditioning of the hands/wrists and adaptation to higher cut/knockdown rates and shorter (two-minute) rounds.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: high spectacle, fast finishes, lower long-term-brain-trauma claims *per fight* (fewer total head impacts than padded multi-round wars — contested). Cons: extreme acute injury (lacerations, hand fractures); thin amateur/development pipeline; limited self-defense breadth.

**Integrates with.** Boxing, MMA striking, and self-defense conditioning.

## Savate / Boxe Française (+ Canne de Combat)

**Root source & history.** Savate grew from early-19th-century Parisian and Marseille street foot-fighting (*chausson*, *savate*). **Michel Casseux ("le Pisseux," 1794–1869)** opened a regulated savate hall and codified rules (no gouging/grappling) c. **1825** (C/B). His student **Charles Lecour (1808–1894)**, after sparring English boxers, fused savate kicks with English-boxing punches c. **1830s** to create **la boxe française** (B). It became a competitive sport with French federation governance in the 20th century.

**Lineage.** *Chausson*/Marseille foot-fighting + Parisian *savate* (Casseux) + English boxing (Lecour) → **boxe française savate** → sibling weapon arts **canne de combat** (single cane) and **bâton** (staff), today governed alongside savate.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** The only striking art that kicks with the *shoe* (pointed toe). Four core kicks — *fouetté* (whip/roundhouse), *chassé* (side/front thrust), *revers* (reverse hook), *coup de pied bas* (low shin kick) — plus boxing hands. Emphasis on mobility, range, precision, and elegant *en garde* footwork. **Canne de combat** is fast, fencing-like single-stick play with circular *moulinets*.

**Training methodology.** *Assaut* (light, points-based, control-emphasis sparring) vs. *combat* (full-contact); glove grades (blue→silver) mark progression. Canne uses fencing masks and light rattan canes.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: long-range striking, footwork, the unusual shoe-kick toolset, and integrated weapon (canne). Cons: no clinch/ground/throws; *assaut* culture can under-pressure-test power; far fewer schools outside France/Europe.

**Integrates with.** Boxing, kickboxing, MMA, Bartitsu (which absorbed savate and canne), and fencing-adjacent stick work.

## HEMA — Historical European Martial Arts (Overview)

**Root source & history.** HEMA is the modern **reconstruction** of European fighting systems from surviving manuals, the oldest being **Royal Armouries MS I.33** (the "Walpurgis"/"Tower" Fechtbuch), a sword-and-buckler treatise dated **c. 1300–1320s, Franconia** (A as a document; the world's oldest surviving European combat manual). Reconstruction means the manuscripts are primary (A) but the living technique inferred from them is scholarly best-effort (B). The HEMA revival is largely a 1990s–2000s phenomenon built on digitized treatises (Wiktenauer).

**Lineage.** Medieval/Renaissance European masters → lost as living lineages (broken transmission) → **rediscovered and reconstructed** from c. 1990s by researchers and clubs → modern HEMA federations and tournaments.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Spans longsword, sword-and-buckler, messer, rapier, dagger, polearms, and unarmed *ringen* (wrestling). Shared logic: timing (initiative/tempo), blade-binding and leverage, footwork, and the principle that the weapon dictates the body.

**Training methodology.** Source interpretation → drilling → **sparring with blunt steel/synthetics and protective gear** (masks, gorgets, gloves, jackets). Tournaments scored on quality/target hits.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: deep weapon literacy, scholarly rigor, growing competitive scene, unique armed skill. Cons: broken-transmission interpretation disputes; gear-heavy and historically niche; limited empty-hand self-defense relevance.

**Integrates with.** Olympic/classical fencing, Jogo do Pau, Bartitsu/canne, and wrestling.

## HEMA — Liechtenauer Longsword (German School / *Kunst des Fechtens*)

**Root source & history.** **Johannes Liechtenauer**, a 14th-century German master (active mid-to-late 1300s) (B/C), encoded his longsword system as the cryptic mnemonic **Zettel (Recital)**. It survives only through later glosses — e.g., the **Codex Döbringer / "Pol Hausen" (Nuremberg Hausbuch, c. 1389)** and the manuscripts of **Sigmund Ringeck, Pseudo-Peter von Danzig, and Paulus Kal** (A as documents). This is the backbone of the "German school" (*Kunst des Fechtens*).

**Lineage.** Liechtenauer (root) → the "Society of Liechtenauer" glossators (Ringeck, Danzig, Kal, Döbringer scribe) → **Talhoffer, Meyer (1570)** and the broader German tradition → modern HEMA longsword.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Two-handed longsword fought from four primary guards (*vom Tag, Ochs, Pflug, Alber*) and five *Meisterhäue* (master cuts): *Zornhau, Krumphau, Zwerchhau, Schielhau, Scheitelhau*. Core principles: *Vor/Nach* (before/after — initiative), *Indes* (the instant, in the bind), *Fühlen* (feeling pressure), and *Winden* (winding at the bind).

**Training methodology.** Gloss study, paired drilling of named plays, and steel/synthetic sparring with mask, gorget, and heavy gloves.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: sophisticated, well-documented blade theory; vibrant tournament longsword. Cons: heavily interpretive (no living master); equipment cost; archaic outside its own context.

**Integrates with.** Italian (Fiore) longsword, ringen, rapier, and fencing.

## HEMA — Fiore dei Liberi / Armizare (Italian School)

**Root source & history.** **Fiore dei Liberi da Premariacco** (born c. 1350; died after 1409), a Friulian knight and master, wrote **Fior di Battaglia / Flos Duellatorum** in the first years of the 15th century, with the briefest redaction dated **1409–1410** (A document). Four manuscript copies survive (Getty, Pisani-Dossi, Morgan, Paris). His art is now called **Armizare** ("art of arms").

**Lineage.** Fiore (root) → 15th-c. Italian tradition → **Filippo Vadi (c. 1480s)** → modern Armizare reconstruction; parallel/competitor lineage to the German school.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** A unified system across **abrazare (wrestling), dagger, sword in one/two hands, poleaxe, spear, and mounted combat**, organized by the famous *segno* (the "Seven Swords" master diagram) and animal virtues (lynx-sight, tiger-speed, lion-courage, elephant-stability). Emphasis on the bind, *coverta* (covering), grappling at the sword, and disarms.

**Training methodology.** Illustrated-play interpretation (Fiore's manuscripts are richly pictorial), paired drills, and protected sparring.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: exceptionally complete, well-illustrated, integrated weapon-and-wrestling curriculum. Cons: same broken-transmission caveats; cross-interpretation debates vs. the German school; niche.

**Integrates with.** Liechtenauer longsword, ringen/abrazare, and dagger work.

## HEMA — Sword & Buckler (MS I.33)

**Root source & history.** The single oldest European fightbook, **Royal Armouries MS I.33** (Franconia, **c. 1300–1320s**) (A document), teaches sword-and-buckler between a *sacerdos* (priest) and *scolaris* (scholar); its final plays feature a woman, **Walpurgis** — among the earliest depictions of a female martial practitioner.

**Lineage.** I.33 (standalone root) → influences/parallels later buckler and "sidesword & buckler" material (e.g., **Achille Marozzo, Bologna, 1536**) → modern I.33 reconstruction clubs.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Seven custodiae (wards) and counter-postures built around a tight "bind-and-thrust" logic where the buckler protects the sword hand. Signature concepts: *obsessio* (besieging a ward), *schiltslac* (shield-strike), and the dominance of the thrust from the bind.

**Training methodology.** Close reading of the 64-page manuscript, ward-by-ward drilling, and light sword-and-buckler sparring (masks, gloves).

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: uniquely old primary source; compact, elegant one-on-one weapon logic. Cons: a single terse manuscript (huge interpretive latitude); very specialized.

**Integrates with.** Liechtenauer/Fiore systems, rapier, and Jogo do Pau-style stick mechanics.

## HEMA — Ringen (German Wrestling Treatises)

**Root source & history.** **Ringen** is documented medieval European grappling, recorded in the same Fechtbuch tradition. Key sources: **Ott Jud**, an early-15th-c. Austrian master whose wrestling appears across many compilations; **Hans Talhoffer's** illustrated manuals (e.g., **1443, 1467**); and **Fabian von Auerswald's *Ringer Kunst*, 1539**, with 85 illustrated techniques (A documents).

**Lineage.** Ott Jud (root master) → Talhoffer, Auerswald, and the wrestling sections of Liechtenauer/Fiore manuscripts → modern HEMA ringen.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Two modes: *geschwind* (sportive, throws and trips) and *kampfringen* (combat — joint breaks, locks, neck cranks, strikes). Throws, hip/leg trips, arm and wrist locks, and grappling tailored to integrate with dagger and sword work.

**Training methodology.** Drilling named "pieces" (*Stücke*) and limited live grappling; serves as the unarmed/closing component of the armed systems.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: rounds out HEMA into a complete system; battlefield-and-judicial-duel context. Cons: less live-tested than judo/wrestling; reconstruction-dependent.

**Integrates with.** Liechtenauer & Fiore systems, dagger, judo/wrestling (modern cross-reference).

## Olympic Fencing (Foil, Épée, Sabre)

**Root source & history.** Sport fencing descends from the **Renaissance Italian** rapier schools and their **French** refinement (16th–18th c.) (B), formalized into a points sport. Fencing appeared at the **first modern Olympics, Athens 1896** (foil and sabre; épée from Paris 1900) (A). The international federation **FIE was founded 29 November 1913** (A), standardizing the three weapons' rules.

**Lineage.** Italian/French historical rapier & smallsword schools → 18th–19th-c. **classical fencing** → **modern electric Olympic fencing** (foil, épée, sabre).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Linear footwork (advance/retreat, lunge, fleche) on a *piste*. Foil (thrust, torso target, right-of-way), épée (thrust, whole body, no priority, first-touch), sabre (cut and thrust, upper body, right-of-way, fastest tempo). Blade actions: parry-riposte, disengage, beat-attack, feint, *flèche*.

**Training methodology.** Individual lessons with a maître, footwork drills, and constant electric-scored bouting — one of the most heavily *competed* arts on this list.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: superb timing, distance, reflexes, and footwork; very safe to spar at full intensity; deep Olympic competition. Cons: highly sportified (priority rules, single weapon line); minimal real-world self-defense or multi-opponent transfer.

**Integrates with.** HEMA rapier (as a cousin), Bartitsu/canne, and any art valuing timing and distance.

## Bartitsu (Victorian Eclectic Self-Defense)

**Root source & history.** **Edward William Barton-Wright** introduced **Bartitsu** in London in **1898** (A — published essays in *Pearson's Magazine*, 1899), a portmanteau of "Barton" + "jujitsu." His **Bartitsu Club** (67b Shaftesbury Avenue, Soho) ran c. **1899–1902**, employing Japanese jujitsu instructors and Swiss cane-master **Pierre Vigny**. Conan Doyle's misspelled "baritsu" — the art Sherlock Holmes used against Moriarty — made it famous (D as the literary myth that outlived the club).

**Lineage.** Jujitsu + English boxing + Savate + **Vigny stick-fighting (la canne)** → **Bartitsu** → revived c. 2000s by the Bartitsu Society; a clear conceptual ancestor of modern eclectic self-defense and an early "MMA."

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Walking-stick/umbrella defense (the Vigny method), jujitsu throws and locks, boxing and savate for striking, and "guard transitions" between weapon and empty-hand ranges — explicitly built for a gentleman in street clothes.

**Training methodology.** Cross-range scenario drills blending the four feeder arts; modern revival is reconstruction-based (from Barton-Wright's articles and Vigny's canne).

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: genuinely integrated multi-range/weapon self-defense concept ahead of its time; the everyday-cane "weapon" is uniquely practical. Cons: historically short-lived; modern form is reconstructed and lightly pressure-tested; more curiosity than competitive art.

**Integrates with.** Jujitsu/judo, boxing, savate, canne de combat, and modern self-defense.

## Jogo do Pau (Portuguese Staff Fighting)

**Root source & history.** **Jogo do Pau** ("game of the stick") is a Portuguese/Galician staff art from the northern provinces (Minho, Trás-os-Montes), used historically to settle feuds and for self-defense — with claims of **500+ years** of documented practice (C/B). It was systematized for sport/duel in the 19th century (the **Pinto/Lisbon urban school**) and survives as living heritage.

**Lineage.** Rural northern Portuguese/Galician feud-and-defense staff use → 19th-c. urban codification (Lisbon masters) → modern federation Jogo do Pau.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** A tall staff (~1.5 m+) wielded with mobile footwork; signature emphasis on **fighting multiple opponents** (a defining feature), circular evasion, and powerful sweeping/thrusting strikes from a constantly moving base.

**Training methodology.** Solo drills, paired forms, and controlled stick sparring; strong oral-lineage transmission.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: rare *living* European weapon tradition (intact lineage, unlike HEMA); genuine multi-opponent methodology; deep cultural roots. Cons: weapon-specific; geographically rare; limited empty-hand and competitive scene.

**Integrates with.** HEMA polearm/staff, canne/bâton, and quarterstaff traditions.

## Defendu (Bridge to Military Combatives)

**Root source & history.** **Defendu** was built by **William E. Fairbairn** (with **Eric A. Sykes**) from his hundreds of street fights as a **Shanghai Municipal Police** officer (joined 1907), synthesizing jujutsu/judo, boxing, savate, and Chinese arts. He published **Defendu (1926)** — where the name first appears — re-issued as *Scientific Self-Defence* (1931) (A). In **WWII** it was distilled into the brutal **close-quarters combatives** taught to British Commandos and the **OSS/SOE** (Fairbairn's *Get Tough!*, 1942).

**Lineage.** Jujutsu + boxing + savate + Chinese methods → **Defendu (Shanghai, 1920s)** → **WWII Allied combatives** → modern military/police defensive-tactics and self-defense systems (a direct ancestor of Western combatives).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Ruthless, gross-motor, "no-rules" simplicity: edge-of-hand ("chin jab," chops), knees, elbows, eye/throat attacks, quick takedowns, and the **Fairbairn–Sykes fighting knife** method — designed to incapacitate fast under stress, not to score.

**Training methodology.** Few high-percentage techniques drilled to reflex under fatigue and aggression; scenario/assault-course conditioning rather than sport sparring.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: maximally practical for lethal close-quarters and military/LEO use; fast to learn; the historical seed of modern combatives. Cons: not a sport (cannot be fully pressure-tested live); ethically and legally severe; limited sporting depth.

**Integrates with.** Boxing, judo/jujutsu, Krav Maga and modern combatives, and edged-weapon defense.
