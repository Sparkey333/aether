# Wrestling & Folk Grappling Worldwide

Wrestling is humanity's most universal combat sport: every settled and nomadic culture independently produced a grappling contest, and cave art at Bnei Hasan, Egypt (~2000 BCE) (A) and Mesopotamian bronze figurines (~2600 BCE) (A) preserve recognizable double-leg and waist-lock positions still scored today. Beneath surface variety the mechanics converge — level change, penetration, hip-to-hip control, off-balancing (kuzushi), and pinning — and the *rules*, not the bodies, define each style: jacket vs. no-jacket, standing-only "touch-the-ground" vs. ground pinning, legal-leg vs. upper-body-only. This cluster maps the great wrestling families, from Olympic codes to living folk traditions, under the Aether principle: keep every layer, label every layer.

A calibration note: empty-hand wrestling scores `rangeWeapon` = 0 by definition and `safetyForSparring` high (8-10), because near-full-resistance live training without strikes is the defining safety advantage of the wrestling family. Pure sport styles score low on `vsArmed`/`vsMultiple` — they are superb *attribute and control* builders but were codified as contests, not battlefield systems.

## Olympic Freestyle Wrestling

**Root source & history.** Freestyle is the direct Olympic codification of British/American **catch-as-catch-can** ("catch wrestling") (B), admitted to the Games at St. Louis 1904 (A). Its 19th-century carnival and collegiate roots make it the youngest *named* code here, but its technical DNA — legal leg attacks, pinning to win — is the oldest, mirroring ancient Greek *pále* and Mesopotamian belt wrestling.

**Lineage.** Lancashire/American catch-as-catch-can → Olympic Freestyle (UWW/FILA codification) → branches: American Folkstyle (collegiate), Iranian *Koshti Azad* (free style), and the takedown game absorbed wholesale into modern **MMA**.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Double-leg and single-leg takedowns, high-crotch, ankle-pick, the "funk" roll, gut-wrench and leg-laces for turns. Scoring rewards exposure (back to mat) and takedowns; matches are two 3-minute periods. Legs are fully legal weapons and targets — the defining contrast with Greco-Roman.

**Training methodology.** High-volume live "go" sparring, drilling chains of attack-reattack, sprawl/scramble conditioning, position-specific situational rounds, brutal anaerobic cardio. Competence to international level is a decade-plus of daily training.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: unmatched takedown skill, real-resistance training, deep talent pool. Cons: minimal submission/strangle work, no striking defense, mat-burn and joint wear; rules reward stalling-avoidance edge cases.

**Integrates with.** The takedown chassis of MMA; cross-trains naturally with Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (top control), Judo (clinch), and boxing (for the "wrestle-box" MMA archetype).

## Greco-Roman Wrestling

**Root source & history.** Despite the romantic name, Greco-Roman is a 19th-century French creation. A Napoleonic-era soldier, **Jean Exbrayat**, codified "flat-hand" (no-fist) fairground wrestling that barred holds below the waist (C/B); the style spread across Europe as *lutte gréco-romaine* and was one of the nine sports of the first modern Olympics, Athens 1896 (A).

**Lineage.** European fairground strongman wrestling → Exbrayat's flat-hand rules → Greco-Roman (UWW code). It is a *rule-restricted offshoot* of the same Continental grappling pool that fed catch.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Upper-body only — no leg attacks, no tripping. Signature high-amplitude throws: the suplex (souplesse), arm-drag to back-take, body-lock, gut-wrench, and the spectacular "reverse lift." Par-terre (ground) wrestling restarts reward turning the down opponent.

**Training methodology.** Heavy emphasis on grip-fighting, posture, and explosive hip throws; "lift" strength training; thousands of suplex repetitions onto crash mats. Among the most physically punishing of all wrestling codes on the lower back and neck.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: devastating clinch throws, elite upper-body control, directly transferable to the MMA/Judo clinch. Cons: no leg defense leaves practitioners exposed to shots; narrow ruleset; high spinal-load injury rate.

**Integrates with.** The clinch and trip-throw game of MMA; complements Judo and Sambo; pairs with striking for "dirty boxing" pummel control.

## American Folkstyle (Collegiate / Scholastic) Wrestling

**Root source & history.** Folkstyle is the North American school-and-college code (B), evolved from catch-as-catch-can carried by British immigrants and frontier "rough-and-tumble," formalized through the NCAA in the 20th century (B). It is the daily wrestling of millions of U.S. students.

**Lineage.** Catch-as-catch-can → American Folkstyle (NCAA/scholastic rules), diverging from Olympic Freestyle on scoring philosophy.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Like freestyle, legs are legal; *unlike* freestyle, it rewards **control and riding** — "riding time," escapes, reversals, and the referee's-position top/bottom restart. Signature: the cradle, the tilt, the leg-ride/turk, the stand-up escape, near-fall (back) points accumulated rather than instant pin-or-nothing.

**Training methodology.** Six-minute (3-period) matches demand grinding pace; folkstyle prizes "mat returns" and breaking an opponent down to control him. Hard live wrestling, weight-cutting culture, and positional top/bottom drilling dominate.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: best-in-world control and "ride" pressure, huge participation base feeding U.S. MMA, builds relentless top game. Cons: insular ruleset (limited international competition), no submissions, weight-cut health toll.

**Integrates with.** Premier feeder to American MMA (pressure top game); blends with BJJ for ground-and-pound and with boxing for the wrestle-box style.

## Catch-as-Catch-Can / Catch Wrestling

**Root source & history.** Catch crystallized in the textile and mining towns of **Lancashire**, England in the 19th century as the least-restrictive, most aggressive English folk style (B). It fused with **Cumberland & Westmorland**, **Devon**, and Irish **collar-and-elbow** styles brought by immigrants and became the carnival "athletic show" wrestling of Britain and America. Its institutional home is **Riley's Gym ("The Snake Pit"), Wigan**, founded by Billy Riley in 1948 (A) — though the tradition long predates it.

**Lineage.** Lancashire/regional English folk wrestling → catch-as-catch-can → (1) Olympic Freestyle and Folkstyle (the "sport" branch, submissions removed); (2) the **"hooking"** submission branch — Billy Riley → Billy Robinson, Karl Gotch → Japanese pro-wrestling "shoot" → modern MMA submission grappling.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Pin *or* submit. Signature "hooks": the double-wristlock (kimura), toe-hold, neck crank, top-wristlock, and the famed Wigan front-headlock series. Relentless top pressure, "ride to break," and joint locks from any position distinguish catch from pin-only sport wrestling.

**Training methodology.** Hard live rolling with submissions, "go behind" and turn-the-corner pressure drilling, conditioning for the carnival gauntlet (beating all comers). Karl Gotch's brutal calisthenics ("Gotch's bible") are still used.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: blends pins and submissions, aggressive top control, the historical bridge into MMA. Cons: fragmented modern lineage, fewer pure schools than BJJ, oral-tradition technique gaps; "hooks" carry real training injury risk.

**Integrates with.** The submission-grappling and clinch core of MMA; cross-pollinates with BJJ (leglocks, kimura), Sambo, and Judo. The historical root of pro-wrestling "shoot" style.

## Sambo & Combat Sambo

**Root source & history.** Sambo (*SAMozashchita Bez Oruzhiya*, "self-defense without weapons") was synthesized in the 1920s–30s USSR from two independent lines: **Viktor Spiridonov**'s softer, injury-economical self-defense system and **Vasili Oshchepkov**'s judo-derived "free wrestling" — Oshchepkov having earned a *nidan* directly under Kanō Jigorō (B). **Anatoly Kharlampiev**, Oshchepkov's student, systematized it from Soviet folk-wrestling styles and lobbied its adoption; the USSR All-Union Sports Committee recognized Sambo as an official sport by **Resolution No. 633 on 16 November 1938** (A).

**Lineage.** Judo + Russian/Caucasian/Central-Asian folk wrestling (Georgian *chidaoba*, Armenian *kokh*, Azerbaijani *gulesh`*, etc.) → Sport Sambo and **Combat Sambo** (strikes added, for military/police). Sport Sambo strongly influenced and is influenced by Judo's leglock and grip game.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Jacket (kurtka) wrestling with shorts and wrestling boots. Famous for **leglocks** (heel hooks restricted in sport, kneebars and ankle locks central), explosive throws, and ground control — but *no chokes in Sport Sambo* (a key contrast with Judo/BJJ). Combat Sambo adds punches, kicks, knees, elbows, and chokes — effectively an early MMA ruleset.

**Training methodology.** Throw-and-pin sport rounds, leglock entanglement drilling, and (for Combat) striking-into-takedown sparring with headgear. Soviet sport-science conditioning underpins it.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: superb throws + leglocks, Combat Sambo is near-complete MMA prep, deep Eastern-European talent. Cons: no sport chokes narrows ground game; access outside the former USSR is patchy; heel-hook drilling risks knee injury.

**Integrates with.** Combat Sambo *is* essentially MMA (Fedor Emelianenko, Khabib Nurmagomedov pedigree); cross-trains with Judo, BJJ (leglocks), and wrestling.

## Shuai Jiao (China)

**Root source & history.** Shuai Jiao is the modern name (coined 1928 by Nanjing's Central Guoshu Academy) (B) for China's ancient jacket wrestling, descending from *jiao li* / *jiao di* (角力/角抵, "horn-butting") of the Zhou dynasty (~1046–256 BCE) (B). Legend ties its origin to the **Yellow Emperor's** battle against Chi You (~2697 BCE) (D) — a foundation myth, not history. Manchu-era *buku* court wrestling shaped the dominant **Beijing/Baoding** styles (C).

**Lineage.** Jiao li / jiao di → regional jacket wrestling → Beijing, Baoding (Hebei), Shanxi, and Mongolian-influenced styles → standardized competitive Shuai Jiao (1950s).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Short jacket and belt; emphasis on **fast, hard throws from standing** with little ground follow-up — points are scored for the throw, and (in many rulesets) a clean throw where you stay standing wins. Signature trips, hip throws, and arm-drag-to-throw integrated with stand-up striking sensibility (it historically sat inside Chinese martial arts as the throwing component).

**Training methodology.** Heavy jacket-gripping drills, "da lun" rotation throws, the *bang zi* (post) and weighted-implement strength work, partner throw repetition onto mats.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: explosive throws, integrates with Chinese striking arts, low ground-injury exposure. Cons: little to no ground or submission game, fragmented schools, limited international competition circuit.

**Integrates with.** Sanda (Chinese kickboxing already incorporates Shuai Jiao throws); complements Judo and any striking art needing a clinch-throw.

## Mongolian Bökh

**Root source & history.** *Bökh* ("durability/wrestling") is among the oldest documented Eurasian wrestling traditions; imagery on Xiongnu-era bronze plaques (206 BCE–220 CE) shows comparable holds (A/C), and it was core to Mongol warrior training under Genghis Khan (1206–1227) (C). It is one of the "Three Manly Games" of the **Naadam** festival, alongside archery and horse racing.

**Lineage.** Steppe nomadic wrestling → regional Mongolian (Khalkha) and Inner-Mongolian (Ölööt) variants, with rule differences in grip and what touches count as a loss.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Standing-only: any body part above the knee touching the ground loses (in Inner-Mongolian Ölööt rules, touching above the ankle loses). **No weight classes, no time limit.** Open-finger *zodog* (jacket) and *shuudag* (briefs) plus boots; grips to the jacket, arms, belt, and legs are legal. Signature trips, leg-hooks, lifts, and the eagle dance (*devekh*) ritual.

**Training methodology.** Strength and grip from herding life, throw repetition, and tournament bracket experience (up to 512/1024 entrants single-elimination). Ranks (e.g., *Nachin* "Falcon" up to *Avarga* "Titan") earned by Naadam rounds won.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: ferocious standing power and balance, open-weight tests pure skill, deep cultural prestige. Cons: no ground game, ritual/grip rules don't transfer cleanly to MMA, regional rule splits.

**Integrates with.** Provides elite trip/throw and balance for any grappler; Mongolian wrestlers have crossed into Judo and sumo (e.g., yokozuna Asashōryū, Hakuhō) and freestyle wrestling.

## Turkish Yağlı Güreş (Oil Wrestling)

**Root source & history.** *Yağlı güreş* is Turkey's national sport, with the **Kırkpınar** festival in Edirne held annually since at least **1346** (A) — widely cited as the world's oldest continuously held sporting competition. UNESCO inscribed it as Intangible Cultural Heritage in **2010** (A). Roots trace to Oghuz Turkic and broader Central-Asian wrestling carried west into Anatolia (C).

**Lineage.** Central-Asian Turkic wrestling → Anatolian oil wrestling → the Kırkpınar competitive tradition (graded by *pehlivan* ranks: *başpehlivan* down to youth tiers).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Wrestlers (*pehlivan*) wear leather *kıspet* trousers and douse themselves in **olive oil**, making conventional grips nearly impossible — control comes via reaching *inside* the kıspet (the *paça* grip) and through the waistband. Win by pinning, lifting and carrying the opponent, or forcing the navel skyward. Matches once ran for hours.

**Training methodology.** Grip and core endurance, oil-conditioned wrestling on grass fields, generational master-apprentice transmission, and a famous accompanying *davul-zurna* (drum-and-pipe) ceremonial culture.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: extraordinary grip/forearm endurance, deep tradition, dramatic spectacle. Cons: the oil ruleset is highly specialized and non-transferable; standing-pin focus, no submissions/ground; niche access outside Turkey.

**Integrates with.** Grip-strength and clinch attributes for any wrestler; little direct technical transfer beyond endurance and waist control.

## Indian/Pakistani Kushti / Pehlwani

**Root source & history.** *Pehlwani* (a.k.a. **Kushti**) is a Mughal-era synthesis (16th c. onward) of native South-Asian **malla-yuddha** — described in the 13th-century *Malla Purana* and rooted far earlier (C) — with Persian **koshti pahlavani** brought by the Turko-Mongol Mughals (B). The names derive from Persian *pahlavani* ("heroic") and *koshti* ("wrestling").

**Lineage.** Malla-yuddha (indigenous) + Persian koshti → Pehlwani/Kushti → practiced in the *akhara* (earthen wrestling school) across North India and Pakistan; the Great Gama (Ghulam Mohammad, undefeated ~50-year career) is its iconic figure (C).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Wrestled on a prepared clay/mud pit (*gara/akhara*), near-nude with a langot. Win by pinning both shoulders (*chit*). Signature: the *dhobi paat* (flying mare/shoulder throw), *kasota*, *dhak*, multiplane throws and pins, with grip on the body (no jacket).

**Training methodology.** A *total way of life*: pre-dawn akhara practice under a *guru/ustad*, mass repetitions of **dand** (Hindu push-ups) and **bethak** (Hindu squats) in the thousands, the *gada* (mace) swing, diet of milk, ghee, and almonds, celibacy discipline. Among the most demanding conditioning regimens in all of wrestling.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: phenomenal functional strength/conditioning, real pinning skill, living guru-lineage depth. Cons: declining patronage vs. mat freestyle, no submissions, austere lifestyle barrier; clay-pit format limits international competition.

**Integrates with.** Conditioning and throw base feed Indian Olympic freestyle (Sushil Kumar, Bajrang Punia lineage); shares Persian ancestry with Iranian Pahlavani.

## Iranian Pahlavani / Koshti-e Pahlavani (Zurkhāneh)

**Root source & history.** Iran's ancient "heroic wrestling," tied to the **Zurkhāneh** ("House of Strength") tradition, fuses pre-Islamic Persian (Parthian/Sassanid) physical culture with later Shia-Sufi mysticism (C); UNESCO inscribed the Zurkhāneh ritual sport **Varzesh-e Bāstāni** (Pahlavani) as Intangible Cultural Heritage in **2010** (A). Iran remains a global freestyle-wrestling superpower, a direct cultural descendant.

**Lineage.** Persian koshti/Varzesh-e Pahlavani → (1) the spiritual Zurkhāneh strength-ritual tradition; (2) **Koshti Pahlavani** (jacketless folk wrestling) → modern Iranian Olympic Freestyle (*Koshti Azad*) dominance.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Folk pin-wrestling on the *gowd* (sunken pit). Win by forcing the back/shoulders down. Technically a precursor and cousin to Pehlwani — body grips, hip throws, leg-trips, and pinning.

**Training methodology.** The Zurkhāneh ritual: rhythmic *morshed* drumming, swinging the **mil** (heavy wooden clubs), *sang* (shield press), *kabbadeh* (iron bow), and *takhteh shena* (push-up board) — a spiritual-athletic gymnasium emphasizing humility (*javanmardi*, chivalry) as much as strength.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: extraordinary functional/club strength, ethical-spiritual depth, direct pipeline to world-class freestyle. Cons: the ritual gymnasium is increasingly ceremonial; folk-wrestling format niche outside Iran; no ground submissions.

**Integrates with.** Foundation of Iran's elite Olympic freestyle program; shares ancestry and conditioning ethos with Indian/Pakistani Pehlwani.

## Icelandic Glíma

**Root source & history.** *Glíma* is Nordic folk wrestling descended from Viking-Age Scandinavian grappling carried to Iceland in the settlement era (~870–930 CE) (C); it appears in the medieval sagas and the *Jónsbók* law code (1325) (B). The trouser-grip form has been Iceland's national sport since **1906** (B).

**Lineage.** Norse/Viking wrestling → three living forms: **Brókartök** (trouser-grip, the sportive/national style), **Hryggspenna** (back-grip, a strength test), and **Lausatök** (loose-grip, the aggressive combat form).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** In Brókartök, wrestlers wear leather harness belts at waist and thighs, grip them, and circle (*stígandi*) upright with constant motion; a fall is losing if anything above the knee/elbow touches. Emphasis on **technique and balance over strength** — the *bragð* (trick/throw) catalog includes hip throws (*mjaðmahnykkur*), heel trips (*hælkrókur*), and cross-buttock throws. *Drengskapur* (sportsmanship) is doctrinally required.

**Training methodology.** Upright continuous-movement sparring, belt-grip throw drilling, and an ethic of fair, balanced contest. Lausatök practitioners drill the harder, freer combat repertoire.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: superb upright balance and throwing technique, low-injury sport form, deep heritage. Cons: belt-grip and standing-only rules transfer poorly to no-gi/MMA; small global footprint; sport form is non-resisting in grip by design.

**Integrates with.** Throw/balance attributes for any standing grappler; Lausatök (the combat form) shares ground with broader self-defense; conceptually akin to Schwingen.

## Swiss Schwingen

**Root source & history.** *Schwingen* ("swinging") is Switzerland's Alpine national wrestling, a herdsmen's pastime documented from the early 17th century (B), with a 13th-century Lausanne Cathedral relief showing the trouser-grip (A). The modern institution traces to the **Unspunnenfest of 1805** (B); it is governed today by the Eidgenössischer Schwingerverband and crowns a *Schwingerkönig* ("Wrestling King").

**Lineage.** Alpine herding-culture wrestling → organized Schwingen (19th-c. festival circuit), a sibling tradition to other European belt/trouser-grip styles.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Fought in a **12-meter sawdust ring**, wrestlers don jute *Schwinghosen* over their clothes and grip the belt/waistband. Win by throwing the opponent onto his back while keeping at least one hand on his shorts. Signature throws (*Schwünge*): the *Kurz* (inside hip), *Brienzer*, *Übersprung*, and *Hüfter*.

**Training methodology.** Grip and lift strength, throw repetition on sawdust, and a festival-circuit (*Schwingfest*) competitive ladder. Famously gentlemanly — winners brush the sawdust off the loser's back.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: powerful belt-grip throws, strong cultural prestige and prize culture, sawdust softens falls. Cons: highly specialized grip ruleset, standing-only/no ground, almost no transfer or footprint outside Switzerland.

**Integrates with.** Belt-grip throw mechanics akin to Glíma, Sumo, and jacket wrestling; little direct MMA crossover.

## Senegalese Laamb

**Root source & history.** *Laamb* (Wolof; *Njom* in Serer, *lutte avec frappe* in French) is West-African folk wrestling rooted in pre-colonial **Serer** initiation and harvest tradition (C); it became Senegal's dominant spectator sport and national passion through the 1960s–70s urbanization of Dakar (B). It is part of the wider West-African *Lutte Traditionnelle* family (Togo's *Evala*, Hausa *Kokowa*).

**Lineage.** Serer/West-African village wrestling → (1) **Lutte simple** (pure wrestling, the international West-African code); (2) **Laamb / lutte avec frappe** — the *uniquely Senegalese* form that **permits bare-fist strikes** during the grappling, blurring into MMA.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Win by throwing the opponent so his back, both hands, both knees, or head-and-hand touch the sand. Powerful single/double-leg lifts, slams, body-locks — *plus* punches in the laamb variant. Pre-fight *bàkk* (mystical preparation, gris-gris charms, marabout ritual) is integral.

**Training methodology.** Sand-pit wrestling, heavy strength and explosive power work, striking integration for laamb fighters, and a star/celebrity professional circuit with large purses (champions like Yékini, Balla Gaye 2, Modou Lô).

**Pros & cons.** Pros: explosive power and slams, the strike-inclusive laamb is functionally a striking-takedown hybrid, huge professional prestige. Cons: little ground/submission game, mystical ritual obscures sport development, heavy reliance on raw athleticism.

**Integrates with.** Laamb's strike-plus-takedown format is a natural MMA on-ramp (Reug Reug / Oumar Kane crossed into the UFC); shares the West-African folk family with Nuba wrestling.

## Nuba Wrestling (Sudan)

**Root source & history.** The **Nuba** peoples of the Nuba Mountains (Kordofan, Sudan) practice a centuries-old wrestling tradition tied to harvest, fertility, and inter-village honor (C); George Rodger's 1949 *National Geographic* photographs of ash-covered Nuba wrestlers became globally iconic (A) and influenced Leni Riefenstahl's documentation.

**Lineage.** Nuba Mountains village wrestling → broader Nilo-Saharan / Nile-valley folk wrestling family (cousin to ancient Nubian and Egyptian belt wrestling depicted at Beni Hasan).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Wrestlers coat themselves in **ash** (ritual and grip), sometimes wearing bells and ornaments; the goal is to throw and **pin the opponent on his back**. Standing leg-trips, lifts, and waist-locks dominate; matches are communal festival events, not points-codified sport.

**Training methodology.** Informal, communal — young men train through village contests, agricultural labor conditioning, and seasonal tournaments rather than formal schools.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: raw throwing power and cultural cohesion, ancient continuity. Cons: minimal formal codification or coaching infrastructure, no ground/submission development, endangered by regional conflict and displacement.

**Integrates with.** Part of the African folk-wrestling continuum with Senegalese Laamb; provides throwing/lifting attributes but limited modern transfer.

## Cornish, Lancashire & Collar-and-Elbow Wrestling (British Isles Folk Family)

**Root source & history.** The British Isles produced a dense cluster of regional jacket and grip styles: **Cornish wrestling** (jacket/canvas, throw-to-the-back, documented for centuries and a point of Cornish identity) (C/B); **Cumberland & Westmorland** (back-hold, chest-to-chest grip behind the back); **Devon** (notorious for shin-kicking in hard boots); **Lancashire** catch-as-catch-can (the loose, leg-legal style that birthed freestyle/catch above); and Irish **collar-and-elbow** (both wrestlers grip collar and elbow, square stance) (B).

**Lineage.** Pre-modern British/Celtic folk wrestling → regional codes (Cornish, Cumberland-Westmorland, Devon, Lancashire) and Irish **collar-and-elbow**, the latter carried by emigrants to colonial America (President Abraham Lincoln wrestled in this style) (C) and feeding, with Lancashire catch, into **American catch-as-catch-can and folkstyle**.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Cornish: jacketed throws scored by the *back* (both shoulder-blades and a hip, or specified "pins") with appointed "sticklers" (referees — origin of the phrase). Cumberland back-hold: lock hands behind opponent's back, first to break grip or touch ground loses. Collar-and-elbow: fixed symmetric grip, trip-and-throw chess. Devon: trips plus brutal shin-kicking.

**Training methodology.** Village-green and fairground contest, jacket-grip throw drilling, and (historically) prize-money tournaments at feasts and revels. Largely heritage-preservation societies today.

**Pros & cons.** Pros: rich technical grip-and-trip variety, historically the seedbed of modern freestyle/catch, strong identity. Cons: mostly endangered heritage practices with tiny modern footprints; standing/back-fall rules don't extend to ground or submissions.

**Integrates with.** The direct ancestral pool of Olympic Freestyle, American Folkstyle, and Catch Wrestling; collar-and-elbow and Lancashire are the historical bridge into the entire Anglo-American wrestling-and-MMA tree.
