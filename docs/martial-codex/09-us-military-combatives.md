# US Military Combatives — Defendu → LINE → MCMAP → MACP → SOCP

US military hand-to-hand doctrine is not a single "art" but a documented century-long lineage of field manuals and named programs, each born from a recognized training gap and each openly borrowing from civilian combat sports. The through-line runs from W.E. Fairbairn's Shanghai street-fighting "Defendu" (A/B) through wartime OSS/Commando systems, the USMC's lethal-only LINE (B), the modular belt-based MCMAP (A), the Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu-anchored Modern Army Combatives Program / MACP (A), and the kit-and-armor-focused Special Operations Combatives Program / SOCP (B). The defining doctrinal tension across all of them — pure killing methods vs. a scalable continuum of force usable in peacekeeping — is the engine that drove each successor.

This codex section provenance-tiers every claim: (A) field manual / primary program document, (B) scholarly or journalistic consensus, (C) practitioner/traditional account, (D) folklore/marketing-myth.

## Defendu (Fairbairn–Sykes, Shanghai Municipal Police)

**Origin & people.** William Ewart Fairbairn (b. 28 Feb 1885, d. 20 Jun 1960) served in the Shanghai Municipal Police (SMP) from 1907 to roughly 1940 (B). From ~1910 he was tasked with training the SMP in hand-to-hand combat, knife fighting, and combat handgunning (B). Fairbairn held a black belt in Kodokan judo and studied jujutsu and Chinese arts, then stripped them to what survived hundreds of real street encounters in one of the era's most violent cities (B/C — the "hundreds of fights" figure is a practitioner/biographical claim, tier C). His system, **Defendu**, was first published in Shanghai in 1926 (*Defendu*, North China Daily News & Herald) (A — primary book). Eric Anthony Sykes was his shooting and close-combat co-developer; together they wrote *Shooting to Live with the One-Hand Gun* (1942) (A).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** Defendu is gross-motor, "go-mad" aggression: edge-of-hand ("chin jab," "tiger's claw") blows, simple locks/throws, releases from holds, and integrated weapon work — knife, baton, and point-shooting pistol craft. It was explicitly *not* a sport; no sparring framework, no ranks, "kill or be killed" intent. Fairbairn also built the SMP Reserve Unit (1925), an early armored riot/SWAT precursor (B).

**The Fairbairn–Sykes fighting knife.** Designed by Fairbairn and Sykes with Wilkinson Sword (consulted Nov 1940); the War Office contracted 1,500 units covertly listed as "hunting knives," first batch of ~50 in Jan 1941 (B). Its slender double-edged "stiletto" blade is optimized to slip between ribs; issued to British Commandos, SAS, Airborne, and SOE; it remains the iconic dagger of WWII raiding forces and lives on commercially as the Applegate–Fairbairn knife (B).

**Borrows from.** Kodokan judo, Japanese jujutsu, Chinese street arts, Western boxing/wrestling, plus pragmatic pistolcraft (A/B).

**Pros.** Fast to teach; ruthless simplicity; gross-motor moves survive adrenaline; the historical taproot of *all* Anglo-American combatives. **Cons.** Lethal-intent only (no force continuum), no live sparring so skills are pressure-untested by modern standards, dated against a trained grappler.

**Integrations.** Directly seeded WWII Commando, OSS, and SOE training; transmitted to the US via Rex Applegate (below).

## "Kill or Get Killed" (Rex Applegate, OSS)

**Origin & people.** Col. Rex Applegate (1914–1998) was developing close-combat courses at Camp Ritchie when "Wild Bill" Donovan recruited him into the OSS to run its close-combat and "point shooting" instruction (B). Donovan directed Applegate to learn everything he could from Fairbairn; Applegate then systematized and Americanized the material for OSS operatives and US troops (B).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** His 1943 manual *Kill or Get Killed* (A — primary document) codified Fairbairn-derived unarmed killing blows, knife work, point shooting, prisoner handling, and crowd/riot control. It became the standard US Western-style close-combat reference for decades and went through many editions.

**Borrows from.** Almost entirely Fairbairn's Defendu, refined by combat feedback from OSS field operatives (B).

**Pros.** Battle-validated; bridged British SMP/Commando methods into US doctrine; the riot-control chapters shaped later law-enforcement practice. **Cons.** Same lethal-only limitation; superseded technically but historically foundational.

**Integrations.** The conceptual ancestor of every later US program; Applegate later co-designed the Applegate–Fairbairn fighting knife (B).

## LINE (USMC, ~1989–1998)

**Origin & people.** **LINE** — a backronym whose expansion appears in circulating variants, most commonly "**Linear Involuntary Neural-override Engagement**" (the prompt's "Linear Infighting…" is one such variant; sources agree it was reverse-fitted to the letters after the name) (B/D — the backronym status is documented (B); the "correct" wording is genuinely unsettled (D)). It was developed by **Ron Donvito** (USMC, ret.), conceived from the 1970s and trialed/refined through the early-to-mid 1980s, then adopted as the Marine Corps' standard hand-to-hand system from roughly 1989 until its replacement around 1998–2002 (B).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** A deliberately small, fixed set of linear techniques engineered to function under worst-case conditions — low light, fatigue, MOPP/gas-mask gear, gross-motor only — each ending in a finishing/lethal sequence targeting the nervous system (B). Strict, repeatable, lethal-default.

**Borrows from.** A pared synthesis of karate, judo, jujutsu, boxing, and prior Marine close-combat material (B).

**Pros.** Trainable to a deployable standard fast; robust under stress/equipment; standardized Corps-wide. **Cons.** Lethal-only with no graduated force — a fatal gap once the 1990s shifted Marines into peacekeeping/MOOTW roles where non-lethal control was required; rigid, little live resistance. That gap directly motivated MCMAP.

**Integrations.** Reviewed in 1996 alongside pugil training and combat-hitting skills; folded into the Marine Corps Close Combat Program (1997–1999), the direct bridge to MCMAP (B).

## MCMAP — Marine Corps Martial Arts Program (2000)

**Origin & people.** Launched summer 2000 as a Commandant's initiative under Gen. James L. Jones (A). Jones tasked LtCol George Bristol and MGySgt Cardo Urso — credited with ~70 combined years of martial-arts experience — to build the curriculum (B). Motto: **"One mind, any weapon"** (A).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** A belt-progression system worn on the utility uniform, integrating three "disciplines" — physical, mental (warrior studies, the force continuum), and character. Five belts with documented minimum training hours (A): **Tan** (27.5 hrs, entry-level, all Marines), **Gray** (+25), **Green** (+25, first instructor-eligible tier), **Brown** (+33), **Black** (+40, with degrees up to 6th). Techniques span strikes, throws, chokes, joint locks, knife/bayonet, ground fighting, and weapons-of-opportunity, all mapped onto a *continuum of force* so the same Marine can detain or kill as the situation dictates — the explicit fix for LINE's lethal-only flaw.

**Borrows from.** Okinawan karate, judo, taekwondo, boxing, jujutsu, plus inherited LINE material; later additions absorbed BJJ-style ground concepts and Muay Thai striking (B).

**Pros.** Scalable force continuum; ties combatives to leadership/character development; universal (every Marine trains it); sustainable belt pipeline. **Cons.** Breadth over depth — many Marines never progress past tan/gray; "character discipline" content draws skepticism as doctrine vs. genuine fighting skill; sparring depth varies by unit.

**Integrations.** Cross-pollinated with Army MACP and SOCP; informally enriched by commercial BJJ/MMA where instructors seek it out.

## MACP — Modern Army Combatives Program (2002; doctrine TC 3-25.150)

**Origin & people.** Built by **Matt Larsen** ("the father of modern combatives"), a former Ranger, beginning in the 2nd Battalion, 75th Ranger Regiment in the mid-1990s after the unit judged existing hand-to-hand training inadequate (B). Rangers trained at the Gracie Jiu-Jitsu Academy in Torrance (Rorion and Royce Gracie made several visits to the battalion) (B). Larsen's manual published as **FM 3-25.150 (2002)**, making it Army-wide doctrine; it was superseded by **TC 3-25.150 (2017)**, which also replaced the older FM 21-150 (A).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** The founding insight: most soldiers won't master a deep art, but *anyone* can learn positional dominance — so MACP anchors on **Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** ground control as the trainable base, then layers the other ranges. A **four-level instructor pipeline** (A/B): **Level I** Basic Combatives (~40 hrs/1 wk — escape, dominant position, basic submissions, the famous "Drills 1–3"); **Level II** Tactical Combatives (~80 hrs/2 wks — adds clinch, takedowns, running the force-on-force/tournament framework); **Levels III–IV** Master Combatives (battalion/brigade master-trainer certification, integrating strikes and weapons). A live-training/competition ladder (the All-Army Combatives Tournament) was used to pressure-test skill.

**Borrows from.** Explicitly: **Gracie/Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** (positional core), **wrestling** (takedowns/control), **judo** (throws), **boxing** and **Muay Thai** (striking/clinch) — a deliberate MMA-style synthesis (A/B).

**Pros.** Evidence-driven (live sparring, competition feedback loop); BJJ base is genuinely learnable and effective 1v1; scalable doctrine with a clear instructor pipeline. **Cons.** Sport/ground emphasis criticized as ill-suited to a battlefield with kit, weapons, and multiple attackers — a soldier who shoots for a double-leg in full gear next to his rifle is exposed; later doctrine and SOCP explicitly course-corrected toward weapons-integrated standing fights.

**Integrations.** Provided the technical/organizational template that SOCP extended; shares DNA with MCMAP; the BJJ pipeline links straight to the commercial grappling world.

## SOCP — Special Operations Combatives Program (Program of Record 2010)

**Origin & people.** Created by **Greg Thompson**, a civilian BJJ black belt and combatives instructor, who after 9/11 was among a small group brought in as Federal defensive-tactics/Air-Marshal instructors at Artesia, NM, then began training Army special operators (B). Debriefing returning operators revealed a recurring pattern — a *surprise close-range attack while in full kit, weapon already in hand* — that the sport-ground MACP model didn't address. SOCP became the first officially designated combatives program for US Army Special Operations Forces, a **Program of Record on 23 Mar 2010** (B).

**Doctrine/curriculum.** Built around the operator as he actually fights: in **body armor and load-bearing kit**, with a **primary weapon (rifle) and sidearm present**. Core themes are **weapon retention** (keeping your own gun), **weapon access/transition** (clearing to pistol or blade when grappled), **fighting against the gun/knife at contact distance**, and **multiple-assailant** survival to create space and re-engage with the weapon. Ground fighting is taught for getting *up and back to the gun*, not for staying down — a pointed inversion of sport BJJ priorities.

**Borrows from.** BJJ (Thompson's base, repurposed for kit), wrestling/clinch control, and crucially **edged-weapon systems** — Filipino **Sayoc Kali** is the most cited blade influence for military knife combatives (C/B). It integrates pistol/carbine retention craft descended conceptually from the Fairbairn–Applegate weapon-integration tradition.

**Pros.** Solves the real special-operations problem (fight in armor, retain/access the weapon, against an armed attacker, possibly multiple); now used across Army/USAF/USMC SOF and federal agencies. **Cons.** Highly context-specific (assumes kit and weapons) so it transfers imperfectly to unarmed civilian self-defense; depth requires the equipment and time most conventional troops lack.

**Integrations.** Standard SOF kit-fighting layer atop MACP; pulls in Sayoc Kali blade work and commercial BJJ; the SOCP trainer (a small fixed-blade worn on the kit for retention/last-ditch use) is a widely fielded hardware spin-off (B).

### The cross-art picture (what modern combatives actually run on)

Modern US combatives is openly a **synthesis of commercial civilian arts** (A/B): **Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** supplies the trainable ground/positional core (MACP, SOCP); **wrestling** the takedowns and top control; **judo** throws; **boxing** and **Muay Thai** the striking and clinch; **Sayoc Kali** the edged-weapon syllabus most cited for military knife work; and the **Fairbairn/Applegate** lineage the original DNA plus weapon integration. Elite units demonstrably cross-train these systems at commercial gyms (B).

### The "classified / black-project martial art" question — an honest note

There is **no publicly documented secret or classified "martial art."** Special-operations units do have closed, sensitive training (specific tactics, techniques, and procedures for hostage rescue, close-quarters battle, etc.), but those specifics are simply *not public* — and this codex will not fabricate them. What *is* documented and analyzable is exactly what is above: the open lineage of field manuals (Defendu → *Kill or Get Killed* → LINE → MCMAP → FM/TC 3-25.150 → SOCP) and the well-attested fact that elite units cross-train commercial BJJ, wrestling, Muay Thai, and Kali. Claims of a hidden, named "deadliest secret government fighting system" are **(D) folklore/marketing-myth**: they recur in advertising and lore, carry no primary documentation, and should be read as legend, not doctrine.
