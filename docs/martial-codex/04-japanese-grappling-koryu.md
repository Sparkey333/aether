# Japanese Grappling, Koryū & the Judo→BJJ Line

This cluster traces the unbroken thread from battlefield **jūjutsu** of the Sengoku period, through Jigorō Kanō's 1882 rationalization into **Kōdōkan Judo**, to its Brazilian mutation into ground-dominant **Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** — plus the parallel aiki branch (Daitō-ryū → Aikido → Hapkido), the ritual-sport of **Sumo**, and the classical weapon arts (kenjutsu/kendō, iaidō, naginata, bōjutsu, kyūdō). The Aether doctrine here is to keep the *grappling spine* visible: every art below either feeds, branches from, or deliberately diverges from the koryū-jūjutsu root, and every transition is dated and tiered.

A note on calibration: stats are scored on a single cross-art scale so a koryū sword art and a modern sport grappling art can be compared on the same axes. "vsArmed" rewards systems that actually train weapon defense; pure sport grapplers score low even when devastating empty-hand.

## Classical Jūjutsu (Koryū: Takenouchi-ryū, Kitō-ryū, Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū)

**Root source & history.** Jūjutsu ("yielding art") is the umbrella for the battlefield close-combat schools of feudal Japan — grappling, joint-locking, throwing, and small-weapon work designed for an armored warrior who had lost or never drew his primary weapon. **Takenouchi-ryū** is conventionally dated to **1532** (Tenbun 1), founded by Takenouchi Hisamori in Mimasaka province, and is widely cited as the oldest surviving jūjutsu koryū (B); its founding origin myth involves a mountain ascetic (yamabushi) revealing techniques in a dream (D). **Kitō-ryū** ("rise-and-fall school") was formalized in the **mid-17th century** (lineage credits Fukuno Shichirōemon and Chinese influence via Chen Yuanbin) and emphasized **nage-waza** (throws) and kata in armor (B/C). **Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū** was synthesized in the **1830s** by Iso Mataemon Masatari from Yōshin-ryū and Shin-no-Shindō-ryū, foregrounding **atemi** (vital-point strikes), chokes, and pins (B).

**Lineage.** Parent traditions: Sengoku battlefield grappling, sumō, and (per tradition) Chinese seizing methods. These three schools are the **direct genetic parents of Judo** — Kanō trained in Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū and Kitō-ryū and explicitly preserved their kata (Itsutsu-no-kata from Tenjin Shin'yō, Koshiki-no-kata from Kitō) (B). Branches: Judo (via Kanō), and indirectly every art downstream of it.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Off-balancing (kuzushi) in armor, sacrifice throws, standing joint locks (kansetsu-waza) to wrist/elbow, atemi to unprotected gaps in armor, and tantō (knife) retention/defense. Kata-based transmission encodes scenarios (seated, standing, armored, armed).

**Training methodology.** Almost entirely **kata** (prearranged paired forms) — there is essentially no free sparring; transmission is master-to-disciple with **menkyo kaiden** (full-transmission license) rather than belt grades. Densho (scrolls) preserve curriculum.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: complete battlefield system including weapons and armor logic; deep historical/cultural depth; techniques live-or-die pragmatic. Cons: no pressure-tested sparring, so practical skill against a resisting opponent is unverified; rare and geographically concentrated; quality varies wildly between authentic koryū and modern reconstructions.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Direct ancestor of Judo, Aikido (via Daitō-ryū's similar lineage), and the weapon koryū; modern practitioners often cross-train kenjutsu and iaidō.

## Judo (Kōdōkan)

**Root source & history.** Founded **February 1882** by **Jigorō Kanō** (1860–1938) at the Eishō-ji temple in Tokyo; the school took the name **Kōdōkan** (B/A — well-documented primary records). Kanō distilled Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū and Kitō-ryū into a **safe, pedagogically systematic** art by removing the most dangerous techniques from randori and emphasizing principles (**seiryoku zen'yō**, maximum efficiency; **jita kyōei**, mutual welfare). The famous 1886 superiority of Kōdōkan over rival jūjutsu schools (often framed as the Tokyo police tournament) is partly historical, partly myth-amplified (C). Judo became an **Olympic sport in 1964** (Tokyo) (A).

**Lineage.** Parents: Tenjin Shin'yō-ryū, Kitō-ryū. Branches: **Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** (via Maeda), **Sambo** (Oshchepkov/Spiridonov absorbed judo), Kosen judo (ne-waza-heavy university variant), and modern Olympic sport judo.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Throws (**nage-waza**: seoi-nage, uchi-mata, o-soto-gari, harai-goshi), pins (osaekomi), chokes (shime-waza), and joint locks (kansetsu-waza, restricted to elbow in sport). Built on **kuzushi-tsukuri-kake** (off-balance, fit-in, execute).

**Training methodology.** Live **randori** (free sparring) is the engine — this is Judo's revolutionary contribution and the reason it eclipsed kata-only jūjutsu. Plus kata for principle, uchikomi (throw entries) for repetition, and a **colored belt (kyū/dan)** system Kanō invented and the world copied.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: brutally effective takedowns; pressure-tested daily; cheap and globally available; Olympic competitive depth; elite balance/breakfall. Cons: sport rules have shrunk leg-grabs and ground time (newaza), narrowing the toolkit; gi-dependent grips; ACL/shoulder injury rate is significant from hard throws.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** The hub of this cluster — feeds BJJ, Sambo, MMA takedowns; cross-trains beautifully with wrestling and BJJ.

## Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu (BJJ)

**Root source & history.** Seeded when **Mitsuyo Maeda** ("Conde Koma," 1878–1941), a Kōdōkan judoka and traveling prizefighter, arrived in Brazil by **November 1914** (A). In **1917** the teenage **Carlos Gracie** saw Maeda demonstrate and began training (with Maeda and/or his student Jacyntho Ferro) (B/C — direct-instruction claims are partly contested by historians). Carlos and especially **Hélio Gracie** adapted the art (~1920s–30s) toward **leverage over strength** and **ground fighting**, founding what became **Gracie/Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu** (B; Gracie marketing inflates some claims to (D)). The **Gracie–Machado** branch (the Machado brothers are Gracie cousins via Carlos's line) modernized teaching, especially in the US.

**Why it diverged from Kanō's judo.** Sport judo rewards the throw (ippon ends the match) and limits ground time; BJJ inverted the emphasis, treating the **takedown as a gateway to the ground**, where positional hierarchy (mount → back → submission) and the **guard** (fighting effectively off one's back) became the art's identity. This is the cluster's key fork: same root, opposite center of gravity (A/B).

**Lineage.** Parent: Kōdōkan Judo (ne-waza, via Maeda). Branches: **sport gi BJJ** (IBJJF Mundials began 1996), **no-gi grappling / ADCC** (inaugural **1998**, Abu Dhabi), and BJJ-as-MMA-base. Sub-styles: Gracie self-defense vs. modern sport/competition jiu-jitsu (leg-lock systems, wrestling-up).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Guard play, sweeps, positional dominance, and submissions: **rear-naked choke, armbar, triangle, kimura, heel hooks** (no-gi/ADCC). Modern meta: leg entanglements, berimbolo, wrestling-up.

**Training methodology.** Heavy live **rolling** (sparring) at near-full resistance, drilling, positional sparring; belt system (white→black, ~10+ years to black) far slower than judo's. Self-defense vs. sport schools differ markedly in curriculum.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: best-tested 1v1 grappling on Earth; lets smaller people control larger ones; safe to spar hard; deep competition scene. Cons: minimal weapon/multiple-attacker training; pulling guard is dangerous on concrete/vs. multiple foes; slow to proficiency; striking absent.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Core ground game of MMA; pairs with wrestling (takedowns), judo (throws), Muay Thai/boxing (striking). The single most cross-trained art in modern combat sports.

## Aikido

**Root source & history.** Developed by **Morihei Ueshiba** (1883–1969) from **Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu**, which he learned under **Takeda Sōkaku** (1859–1943) beginning 1915. The name **"aikido"** was officially registered in **1942** (B). Daitō-ryū's own claimed descent from the Minamoto/Takeda clans back to Prince Teijun is lineage legend (D); its real documented history begins with Takeda (B). Ueshiba infused his postwar art with Ōmoto-kyō spirituality, shifting it toward harmony and non-violence.

**Lineage.** Parent: Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu (which shares the broad jūjutsu root). Branches: **Aikikai** (Ueshiba family mainline), **Yoshinkan** (harder, Shioda), **Tomiki/Shodokan** (competitive, randori), **Ki Society** (Tohei). Cousin: **Hapkido** (see below), which descends from the same Daitō-ryū source.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Blending with an attacker's momentum; wrist and joint controls (**ikkyo–yonkyo, kote-gaeshi, nikyo**), throws (**irimi-nage, shihō-nage, kokyū-nage**), and ukemi (breakfalls). Circular, off-balancing, weapon-derived movement (aiki-ken, aiki-jō).

**Training methodology.** Cooperative paired practice; uke attacks committedly and is thrown — **no competitive resistance** in mainline styles (Tomiki excepted). Emphasis on flow, posture, and blending over winning.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: excellent for joint-control, falling safely, de-escalation ethos, and weapon-aware footwork; low injury, lifelong practice. Cons: the cooperative training model means real-fight efficacy is heavily disputed; struggles against a non-telegraphed, resisting, or grappling opponent; little pressure-testing.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Shares DNA with Daitō-ryū and the jūjutsu koryū; weapon work overlaps kenjutsu/jōjutsu; practitioners seeking realism often add judo or BJJ.

## Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu

**Root source & history.** Brought to public prominence by **Takeda Sōkaku** in the late 19th–early 20th century; claimed ancient Takeda-clan provenance is legend (D), with verifiable history starting at Takeda (B). It is the **aiki branch** of jūjutsu — emphasizing internal off-balancing (**aiki**) to neutralize an opponent's structure before technique.

**Lineage.** Parent: classical jūjutsu / Takeda clan martial tradition (claimed). Branches: **Aikido** (Ueshiba), **Hapkido** (Choi, contested), and several Daitō-ryū lines (Kondō/Sōke line, Takumakai, Kodokai, Roppokai).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** A vast catalogue (hundreds of techniques across ikkajō–gokajō scrolls): pins, joint locks, throws, and the elusive **aiki-age/aiki-sage** structural manipulations. More combatively oriented and technically denser than its Aikido offshoot.

**Training methodology.** Kata-based paired transmission, menkyo licensing; very little free sparring, like other koryū.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: technically deep, the wellspring of two major arts, strong joint-control and aiki concepts. Cons: rare and esoteric; aiki claims hard to verify or pressure-test; lineage authenticity debated; minimal live resistance.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Direct parent of Aikido and Hapkido; conceptual overlap with jūjutsu koryū and judo's kuzushi.

## Hapkido

**Root source & history.** Korean hybrid art founded by **Choi Yong-sool** (1904–1986), who returned to Korea after WWII teaching techniques characteristic of **Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu** (B). Whether Choi was a formal Daitō-ryū student under Takeda is **contested**: Takeda's enrollment records (eimeiroku, released by Kondō Katsuyuki) show only brief contact, and many scholars credit Yoshida Kōtarō as a likely intermediary (B/C). The name "hapkido" (= 合氣道, same characters as aikidō) emerged in the 1950s; Korean nationalist narratives sometimes overstate indigenous origins (D).

**Lineage.** Parent: Daitō-ryū Aiki-jūjutsu (via Choi). Influenced by/blended with Korean kicking (Tang Soo Do, Taekwondo) and later Yusul/Yawara. Branches: numerous (Sin Moo Hapkido, Combat Hapkido, Hankido).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Joint locks and throws (Daitō-ryū-like) **plus dynamic Korean kicks**, pressure points, weapons (cane, short stick, sword, rope), and circular redirection. Broader striking range than Aikido.

**Training methodology.** Paired technique drilling, some sparring (varies hugely by school), belt grading. Eclectic curriculum spanning kicks, locks, throws, and weapons.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: well-rounded self-defense mix of striking, locking, throwing, and weapons; practical joint-control; accessible globally. Cons: enormous quality variance between schools; many techniques cooperatively trained; against a trained grappler or boxer, efficacy depends heavily on the school's sparring culture.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Shares aiki-jūjutsu root with Aikido; pairs with Taekwondo (kicks); self-defense practitioners blend it with judo/BJJ for ground gaps.

## Sumō

**Root source & history.** Japan's national sport and oldest documented grappling tradition. The **Nihon Shoki** (compiled 720 CE) records a legendary first bout between **Nomi no Sukune** and **Taima no Kuehaya** dated to **23 BCE** — folklore origin (D). The first **historically attested** matches were court entertainment in **642 CE** before a Korean legation (B). Sumō evolved from Shintō harvest ritual into Heian-court ceremony, then street/temple spectacle, into the **professional ōzumō** of the Edo period (~1684 onward, the lineage of today's Japan Sumo Association) (B/A).

**Lineage.** Parent: ancient Shintō ritual wrestling. Influenced early jūjutsu's grappling vocabulary. Branches: amateur/collegiate sumō (international, now in many countries), and modern professional ōzumō.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Win by forcing the opponent **out of the ring (dohyō)** or making any part above the sole touch the ground. **48 classical kimarite** (winning techniques) — pushes (oshi), thrusts (tsuki), throws (nage), belt grips (yotsu). Explosive **tachi-ai** (initial charge) is decisive.

**Training methodology.** Brutal **heya (stable)** life: thousands of daily reps (shiko stomps, teppō pole-strikes, butsukari-geiko collision drills), full-contact practice bouts, weight gain via chanko-nabe. Rank by tournament results (banzuke), not belts.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: world-class explosive power, balance, and hand-fighting; tested against full resistance daily; immense cultural depth. Cons: ruleset (ring-out, no ground game) limits broad self-defense transfer; extreme physical demand and joint wear; closed professional system, hard to access outside Japan/amateur clubs.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Conceptual cousin to jūjutsu/judo gripping and off-balancing; sumō converts have excelled in judo and MMA (clinch, takedown power).

## Kenjutsu & Kendō

**Root source & history.** **Kenjutsu** ("sword technique") is the classical battlefield/dueling swordsmanship of the koryū, proliferating after the **Ōnin War (1467–77)** (B); famous schools include Kashima Shintō-ryū, Itto-ryū, and Yagyū Shinkage-ryū. **Kendō** ("way of the sword") is its modern, sportified descendant: the **shinai** (bamboo sword) and **bōgu** (armor) developed in the 18th–19th centuries enabled full-speed sparring; the term "kendō" was standardized around **1919** (Nishikubo Hiromichi) under the **Dai Nippon Butokukai** (founded 1895). Banned during the 1945–52 Occupation, revived with the **All Japan Kendo Federation in 1952** (B/A).

**Lineage.** Parent: Sengoku-era kenjutsu koryū. Branch: modern sport **kendō**; sibling: **iaidō** (drawing). Many koryū kenjutsu lines survive in parallel.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Two-handed straight-cut swordsmanship; kendō scores valid strikes (men, kote, dō, tsuki) with spirit-sword-body unity (**ki-ken-tai-ichi**). Kenjutsu emphasizes kata, timing, and lethal application.

**Training methodology.** Kenjutsu = kata with bokutō. Kendō = armored **shiai/jigeiko** sparring plus suburi and kata. Dan grading in kendō; menkyo in koryū.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: superb timing, distance, and decisiveness; kendō is intensely pressure-tested; lifelong, deeply cultural. Cons: shinai/sport conventions diverge from real blade mechanics; empty-hand and ground gaps; armor and equipment cost; limited direct unarmed self-defense transfer.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Pairs with iaidō (draw + cut), jōjutsu, and naginata; conceptual overlap with all koryū footwork and timing.

## Iaidō (Iaijutsu)

**Root source & history.** The art of **drawing the sword and cutting in one motion** from the sheathed position, classically **iaijutsu**, traditionally traced to **Hayashizaki Jinsuke Minamoto no Shigenobu** (~16th century) (C). The modern unified form, with the **All Japan Iaido Federation founded in 1948** and the standardized Zen Ken Ren seitei-iai set, is gendai budō (B). Toyama-ryū (a simplified military iai) dates to **1925** (B).

**Lineage.** Parent: kenjutsu koryū (Musō Jikiden Eishin-ryū, Musō Shinden-ryū). Sibling: kendō. Branch: modern seitei iaidō.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** **Nukitsuke** (draw-cut), **kirioroshi** (downward finishing cut), **chiburi** (blood-shake), **nōtō** (resheathing). Solo kata performed against imagined opponents.

**Training methodology.** Almost entirely **solo kata** with live (shinken) or blunt (iaitō) blades; intense focus on precision, posture, and mental composure (zanshin). No sparring.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: exquisite precision, etiquette, and mental discipline; preserves authentic sword craft; low injury, lifelong. Cons: no resisting opponent, so combative realism is theoretical; niche and equipment-dependent; near-zero unarmed/modern self-defense application.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Natural pair with kendō (kendōka study iaidō for blade authenticity) and kenjutsu koryū.

## Naginata-dō (Naginatajutsu / Atarashii Naginata)

**Root source & history.** The **naginata** (glaive/halberd) descends from earlier pole-arms (hoko yari) of the late 1st millennium and was a battlefield weapon of the Heian–Sengoku periods, famously associated with sōhei (warrior monks) and, in the Edo period, with **women of the samurai class** for home defense (B). Classical **naginatajutsu** koryū (e.g., Tendō-ryū, Jikishinkage-ryū) persist; in the **Shōwa era** it entered girls' school physical education as **naginata-dō**, and postwar reformed into competitive **atarashii naginata** (B).

**Lineage.** Parent: Sengoku pole-arm combat / koryū naginatajutsu. Branch: modern sport atarashii naginata (predominantly practiced by women).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Long-weapon sweeps and thrusts exploiting **reach and leverage**; strikes to shin (sune), men, kote, dō, and tsuki; spinning and switching grips. Excellent against shorter weapons via distance control.

**Training methodology.** Kata (koryū) and armored sparring (atarashii naginata, with bōgu and a bamboo-bladed naginata); dan grading in the modern form.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: superb reach, footwork, and timing; pressure-tested in the modern sport form; rich female-lineage history. Cons: weapon-specific and rarely available outside Japan/dedicated clubs; little empty-hand transfer; equipment cost.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Sibling of kendō within the budō family; conceptual overlap with bōjutsu and spear arts.

## Bōjutsu (Staff Arts)

**Root source & history.** The art of the **rokushakubō** (six-foot staff) and shorter sticks (jō, hanbō), among the oldest and most universal Japanese weapon practices; integral to many jūjutsu and bujutsu koryū and to Okinawan kobudō (B). Staff methods appear across Sengoku-era schools and temple traditions; the related **jōjutsu** (four-foot staff) is famously codified in **Shintō Musō-ryū** (~early 17th c., founder Musō Gonnosuke) (B/C — the duel-with-Musashi origin is legend, D).

**Lineage.** Parent: koryū weapon traditions and folk staff fighting. Related: jōjutsu (Shintō Musō-ryū), hanbōjutsu, Okinawan bōjutsu. Often a module within larger jūjutsu/ninjutsu/aiki systems.

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** Strikes, thrusts, blocks, sweeps, and joint-trapping with a non-edged staff; both ends live. Leverage and range against sword and unarmed attackers; jō integrates against the katana.

**Training methodology.** Paired kata and drills; some styles spar with padded staves. Often trained alongside empty-hand or sword curricula rather than standalone.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: a staff is an improvised-weapon-friendly skill (broom, cane, pole); strong range and leverage; low lethality for control. Cons: kata-heavy with limited live testing in most lines; weapon access in daily life is situational; standalone bōjutsu schools are uncommon.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Embedded in jūjutsu koryū, aikidō (aiki-jō), and Okinawan kobudō; jōjutsu pairs directly with kenjutsu.

## Kyūdō (Japanese Archery)

**Root source & history.** The "way of the bow," descended from **kyūjutsu**, the samurai (and earlier court/ceremonial) archery of feudal Japan using the asymmetric **yumi** longbow (B). Mounted archery (yabusame) and ceremonial/Zen-influenced foot archery are ancient (the bow predates the sword in Japanese warrior culture). Modern **kyūdō** is gendai budō, formalized under the All Nippon Kyudo Federation (1949) with standardized shooting protocol (B).

**Lineage.** Parent: kyūjutsu (Heki-ryū, Ogasawara-ryū ceremonial archery). Branches: modern kyūdō styles (Heki-ryū target lines, Ogasawara ceremonial), yabusame (mounted).

**Core mechanics & signature techniques.** The **hassetsu** (eight stages of shooting) — a precise, meditative sequence from stance to release (hanare) and follow-through (zanshin). Emphasis on form and spirit over score in many schools ("standing Zen").

**Training methodology.** Repetition of form, often shooting at a makiwara (close straw target) before the 28-meter target (mato); strong ritual and etiquette. Solo and rank-graded; no opposition.

**Honest pros & cons.** Pros: profound focus, posture, breath, and mental discipline; beautiful, lifelong, low-injury practice; living link to samurai culture. Cons: essentially **zero interpersonal combat application** today; equipment- and space-dependent; niche availability.

**Integrates / cross-trains with.** Spiritually and culturally allied with the other "dō" budō (kendō, iaidō); least overlapping with the grappling spine of this cluster.
